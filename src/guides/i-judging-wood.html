Builders, furniture makers and carvers all ask the same questions about a piece of wood: Will it dent? Will it sag? Will it stay flat? Will it rot outside? Will it cut cleanly? This guide explains the numbers and ratings that answer those questions. Every species page in uses them.

Key points

  • Hardness (Janka) is dent resistance. Strength is how much load it takes to break. Stiffness is how much it sags. They are three different things.
  • Wood shrinks about twice as much along the growth rings (tangential) as across them (radial). That is why flatsawn boards cup.
  • Only heartwood can be rot resistant. The sapwood of every species rots.
  • Carvers want soft, even wood; turners want dense, fine wood; builders want stiff, straight, cheap wood.
  • All fine wood dust is harmful. Some species also cause allergies.

Each page in Guide J describes one wood. From top to bottom, a page has:

  • A sample: the flatsawn face and the end grain, drawn in the wood’s real colour.
  • Facts: names, colour, grain and texture, figure (decorative patterns such as curl or ray fleck), how it changes with age, where to buy it in Calgary, relative price ($ to $$$$), and dust hazards.
  • Numbers: averages at 12% moisture content from the US Forest Products Laboratory and the Wood Database. A single board can be 20% or more above or below the average.
  • Property meters (1–5): the numbers turned into bars using the cut-offs below, so you can compare woods at a glance.
  • Working ratings (1–5): how well it takes hand tools, machining, carving, turning, nails and screws, glue, finishes and steam bending. These are this handbook’s judgement from published working notes: 1 = fights you, 5 = a pleasure.
  • Strengths, weaknesses, uses for builders and for carvers and turners, and one shop tip.

Softwood comes from conifers (needle trees); hardwood comes from broadleaf trees. The names are botanical, not a hardness rating: basswood and aspen are hardwoods but softer than Douglas fir.

How the property meters are scored
Worked example

Reading Douglas fir’s numbers: Janka 620 lbf, bending strength 12,500 psi, stiffness 1.77 million psi, volumetric shrinkage 11.6%, moderately durable heartwood.

  1. Hardness: 620 is in the 500–800 band → 2. It dents fairly easily.
  2. Strength: 12,500 is in the 10,500–13,000 band → 3.
  3. Stiffness: 1.77 is in the 1.70–1.90 band → 4. Stiff, which is why it is used for beams.
  4. Stability: 11.6% is in the 10.5–12.5 band → 3. Rot: moderate → 3.

Stiff and fairly strong but easily dented. Good for beams, joists and stringers; poor for tabletops unless you like a worn look.

Common mistake

Treating a 5 as “best”. The meters compare these 25 woods only, and more isn’t always better: a carver wants a low hardness score, and hickory’s top marks for hardness come with a 1 for carving.

Three numbers describe how wood takes a load:

  • Janka hardness: the force, in pounds (lbf), needed to press a 0.444″ steel ball halfway into the wood. It predicts dent resistance: floors, tabletops, stair treads, workbenches.
  • Bending strength (modulus of rupture, MOR): how much stress the wood takes in bending before it breaks, in psi. It matters for anything that could snap: ladders, handles, beams at their limit.
  • Stiffness (modulus of elasticity, MOE): how much it resists bending, in millions of psi. It sets how far a shelf, joist or beam sags long before anything breaks.
Formula
sag ∝ load × span³ ÷ (MOE × width × thickness³)
sag A ÷ sag B = MOE B ÷ MOE A

The second line compares two woods of the same size, span and load. Thickness is cubed, so a little extra thickness helps a lot.

Worked example

A 36″ bookshelf, ¾″ thick. White pine (MOE 1.24) or hard maple (MOE 1.83)?

  1. Pine sags 1.83 ÷ 1.24 = 1.48 times as much as maple.
  2. Make the pine 1″ thick instead: (0.75 ÷ 1)³ = 0.42, so it sags less than half as much as at ¾″.

A 1″ pine shelf sags less than a ¾″ maple one (1.48 × 0.42 = 0.62 of it). Thickness beats species.

Common mistake

Assuming hard means strong. Douglas fir has half the Janka hardness of red oak (620 vs 1,290) but almost the same stiffness (1.77 vs 1.82) at about 70% of the weight. That is why beams are fir, not oak.

Green wood is full of water. It only starts to shrink once it dries below the fibre saturation point, about 28% moisture content. From there to bone dry it shrinks by the percentages on each species page:

  • Radial shrinkage (R): across the growth rings, from the pith toward the bark.
  • Tangential shrinkage (T): along the growth rings. Usually about twice R.
  • Volumetric (V): the total change in volume. Lower is more stable.

The T/R ratio predicts distortion. Near 1 (birch 1.3, walnut and mahogany 1.4), the board shrinks evenly and stays true. Above 2 (white pine 2.9), flatsawn boards cup and twist more as they dry.

Formula
C ≈ S ÷ 2800
change in width ≈ W × C × ΔMC

S = the shrinkage percentage in the board’s width direction: T for flatsawn, R for quartersawn. The wood shrinks S% over about 28 points of moisture, so each point is S ÷ 28 percent. W = width; ΔMC = change in moisture content, in points. This is the formula with C worked out from the species page.

Worked example

A 7¼″ red oak board (T 8.6%, R 4.0%) goes through a 4-point moisture swing between summer and winter.

  1. Flatsawn: 7.25 × (8.6 ÷ 2800) × 4 = 0.089″ ≈ ³⁄₃₂″
  2. Quartersawn: 7.25 × (4.0 ÷ 2800) × 4 = 0.041″ ≈ ¹⁄₃₂″

Quartersawn stock moves less than half as much. Choose it for wide panels, doors and anything that must stay flat.

In Calgary

Indoor air in a Calgary winter often sits at 20–30% relative humidity, which dries wood to about 5–6% moisture content. Kiln-dried framing lumber can arrive at up to 19% and keeps shrinking in the house; hardwood from humid regions keeps shrinking too. Let wood sit in the room where it will live for one to two weeks, and check it with a moisture meter before you build.

Common mistake

Building a wide top from a high-shrinkage flatsawn wood (beech: V 17.2%) in summer and fixing it tight. By January it has shrunk and split. Leave room for movement, or pick a stable species.

Rot is caused by fungi, and fungi need water. Wood kept below about 20% moisture content does not rot, which is why indoor framing lasts centuries. Outdoors, what matters is how long the wood stays wet.

A tree has two zones. Sapwood is the living outer band that carries sap; it is pale and rots quickly in every species. Heartwood is the dead core; in durable species it is full of natural chemicals that resist rot and insects. The rot rating on each species page (durability) is for heartwood only.

Choosing for outdoors
  • Very durable or durable heartwood: yellow cedar, teak, black walnut, western red cedar, white oak, genuine mahogany, black cherry.
  • Moderate: Douglas fir, western larch, eastern white pine, sapele. Keep them painted or out of standing water.
  • Low or perishable: spruce, lodgepole pine, hemlock, the maples, red oak, ash, birch, hickory, beech, poplar, aspen, basswood, butternut.
  • Touching the ground: no untreated wood. Use pressure-treated lumber labelled for ground contact (use category UC4.1 or higher under CSA O80).
In Calgary

Calgary is dry, sunny and high, with many freeze-thaw cycles. Outdoor wood here more often fails from sun (greying and surface checks) and from water trapped at joints and end grain than from constant wet. Seal cut ends, leave gaps so wood can dry, and use stainless or hot-dip galvanized fasteners with cedar, oak and pressure-treated lumber.

Common mistake

Using red oak outdoors because oak is “tough”. Red oak’s open pores wick water deep into the board and it rots quickly. White oak’s pores are plugged by growths called tyloses, which is why it is used for barrels and boats.

Sculpting wood is a different test from building with it. What matters:

  • Even texture. In many softwoods the earlywood (the pale, soft part of each ring, grown in spring) is much softer than the latewood (the dark, dense band grown in summer). A gouge dives into one and skips on the other. Even woods cut the same everywhere.
  • Fine texture and small pores. Diffuse-porous woods (maple, cherry, basswood) have tiny pores spread evenly and hold crisp detail. Ring-porous woods (oak, ash, hickory) have a band of large pores in each ring and crumble at fine edges.
  • Hardness to match the tool. Knives and gouges want soft wood (under about 600 lbf). The lathe wants dense wood (about 900 lbf and up) that cuts to a smooth, crisp surface instead of tearing.
  • Straight, predictable grain. Interlocked grain (sapele, some mahogany) switches direction in alternating bands and tears out whichever way you cut.
  • Looks. Figure; chatoyance, a shimmer that moves as you tilt the piece; and spalting, dark lines drawn by fungi in partly rotted wood (beautiful, but soft in places).
Quick picks
  • Whittling, relief and figure carving: basswood, butternut, eastern white pine, yellow cedar, genuine mahogany.
  • Spindle turning: hard maple, cherry, black walnut, beech, yellow birch.
  • Outdoor sculpture: western red cedar, yellow cedar, white oak. Expect surface checks as it weathers.
  • Large glued-up blocks: an even, stable wood (basswood, mahogany) with all the grain running the same way.
Common mistake

Carving “uphill” against the grain. The fibres lift and split ahead of the tool. Cut downhill, from high points toward low points, so the fibres you cut are supported by the wood beneath them.

Fine wood dust is harmful to breathe whatever the species. Hardwood dust is classed as a cause of nasal cancer (IARC Group 1) with long exposure; oak and beech are the most studied. Some woods are also sensitizers: the more you are exposed, the stronger your allergic reaction becomes (rashes, runny nose, asthma), sometimes for life.

Carvers and turners sand a lot, so they breathe more dust than framers. The species below are the ones in this handbook with a specific warning.

Species with a dust warning
Safety
  • Collect dust at the source: a dust collector or shop vacuum on the saw, sander or lathe.
  • Wear a fitted respirator (N95 or better; P100 for cedar and sanding) whenever you sand or machine.
  • Clean up with a vacuum, not a broom, which lifts the finest dust into the air.
  • If a wood gives you a rash or a cough, stop using it. Reactions get worse with each exposure.
Common mistake

Trusting a paper dust mask with cedar. Western red cedar dust contains plicatic acid, a known cause of occupational asthma. A loose mask lets fine dust in around the edges.
