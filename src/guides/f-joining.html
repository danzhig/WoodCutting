A perfectly cut miter still has to be held together. Miters glue end grain to end grain, which is the weakest glue joint in woodworking, so they need help. This short guide covers how to glue, reinforce, fasten and clamp angled joints, and why wide miters open with the seasons.

Key points

  • Size end grain with a first coat of glue, then glue again.
  • A glued miter alone is weak; add a spline, key, biscuit, dowel or screw.
  • Dry-fit and clamp everything before glue goes on.
  • Wide solid-wood miters open at the inside corner when the wood shrinks.

Wood glue is strongest on long grain, where the fibres lie side by side. A miter face is mostly end grain: open fibre tips that wick glue away like a bundle of straws, leaving a dry, starved joint.

The fix is to size the end grain: brush on a thin first coat of glue, let it soak in for a few minutes, then apply a normal coat and clamp.

  1. Dry-fit and set up the clamps first.
  2. Brush a thin coat on both miter faces. Wait 3–5 minutes.
  3. Brush a second, normal coat. Assemble, clamp, and wipe squeeze-out with a damp rag.
Worked example

A picture frame glued with a single coat versus a sized joint.

  1. Single coat: the faces look dry within minutes; the joint breaks with light hand pressure after curing.
  2. Sized: a thin glue line stays wet; the joint is noticeably stronger, though still weaker than a reinforced one.
Common mistake

Relying on glue alone for anything that carries a load. A glued miter is fine for a small picture frame; a door frame or box that gets handled needs reinforcement ().

Reinforcements bridge the joint with long grain or metal:

  • Spline: a thin strip of wood glued into matching slots cut in both miter faces. Invisible if stopped short of the edge.
  • Key (or feather): a slot cut across the outside corner after assembly, filled with a thin slip of wood, often a contrasting colour.
  • Biscuit: an oval wafer of compressed wood in slots cut by a biscuit joiner.
  • Dowels: round pins across the joint, in drilled holes.
  • Pocket screws: angled screws from the back of a frame, hidden when it hangs on the wall.
Worked example

Keys for a ¾″ × 2½″ frame, cut on the table saw with a 45° cradle jig.

  1. Blade height about ⅝″, so the slot reaches well across the joint line.
  2. Two slots per corner, ½″ in from each face. Glue in ⅛″ slips, flush-trim when dry.
Common mistake

Cutting spline slots too deep toward the inside corner so they break through. Stop the slot at least ¼″ from the inside edge, or run it through deliberately and plan for it to show.

Trim miters are held mainly by nails into the wall, but the corner itself needs fixing too, or it will open as the house moves. Glue the miter faces, then drive a thin brad or pin nail through the corner from each side so the nails cross the joint.

Worked example

Door casing head joint: glue the miter, then one 1¼″ brad down through the top of the head into the leg, and one from the side of the leg into the head.

Common mistake

Nailing too close to the tip of the long point. It splits. Keep brads at least ¾″ back from the point, and angle them slightly away from it.

Miters slide under clamp pressure because the faces are at 45° to the force. Three ways to hold them closed:

  • Band clamp: a strap around the whole frame with corner blocks, tightened with a ratchet. It pulls all four corners closed at once.
  • Corner clamps: hold one corner at 90° while the glue sets.
  • Tape trick: for small boxes, lay the sides face down end to end, tape across the joints, flip, glue, and roll them up. The tape is the hinge and the clamp.
Worked example

A 12″ square frame with a band clamp.

  1. Dry-fit with the band on. Check the diagonals ().
  2. Glue, re-clamp, check diagonals again. Push the long diagonal’s corners together if needed.
Common mistake

Over-tightening, which squeezes out all the glue and can bow the frame. Snug is enough; the corners should close with a thin glue line.

Wood shrinks across its width but not its length (). In a mitered corner, the grain of the two pieces runs in different directions, so as each piece narrows, the angle of its miter face changes slightly. When the wood dries and shrinks, the joint opens at the inside corner (the heel); when it swells, it opens at the outside tips.

Narrow miters (casing, picture frames) move too little to notice. Wide ones (a mitered tabletop edge, a box made from wide boards) can open a visible gap.

Formula
gap at the heel ≈ 1.4 × change in width

Worked example

A mitered 1×8 (7¼″ wide), C = 0.003, moisture drops 4 points.

  1. Width change: 7.25 × 0.003 × 4 = 0.087″
  2. Heel gap: 1.41 × 0.087 = 0.12″ ≈ ⅛″

Visible. Use plywood or MDF for wide mitered parts, keep solid-wood miters under about 4″, use quartersawn stock, or choose a different joint.

Common mistake

Cutting and gluing wide miters in a humid shop and bringing them into a heated house. Let the wood acclimatize in the room where it will live for a week or two before final cuts.
