Framing is where all the earlier guides come together at full scale: tape and square, pitch and plumb cuts, and a lot of repeated 2× cuts. Code numbers here are from the National Building Code – 2023 Alberta Edition (Part 9, housing), which applies in Calgary. The code is written in millimetres; the inch values are conversions rounded to the safe side. The City of Calgary issues permits and has the final word, and structural sizes (headers, joists, rafters) come from the code’s span tables or your plans, not from this guide.

Key points

  • 16″ on center: first layout line at 15¼″, then every 16″. Sheet edges land on stud centers.
  • 92⅝″ precut studs + three 1½″ plates = a 97⅛″ wall, right for an 8-foot ceiling.
  • Header length = rough opening + 3″.
  • Rafter: run = span ÷ 2 − ridge ÷ 2; length = run × slope factor. Plumb cut = slope angle.
  • Stairs: risers = total rise ÷ 7½, rounded up; treads = risers − 1; drop the stringer by one tread thickness.

Code values quoted are from the National Building Code – 2023 Alberta Edition, in force in Calgary since May 1, 2024. Check with the City of Calgary before building; permits are required for most structural work.

Walls are laid out on their plates before any stud is cut. Lay the bottom plate and the top plate side by side, edges flush and ends aligned, and mark both at once (ganging the plates). Then every stud lands in the same place top and bottom.

Framers use a small set of marks: a line on one side of the stud position and a letter on the side the stud goes:

  • X: common stud. K: king stud. J: jack (trimmer) stud. C: cripple.
  • Mark the crown of every stud and joist and install them crown-the-same-way ().
Worked example

A wall with a window between the 3rd and 5th stud positions.

  1. Mark the regular layout (X) along the full plate first.
  2. Mark the window’s king (K) and jack (J) studs from the rough opening, and change any X that falls inside the opening to C (cripple).
Common mistake

Putting the X on the wrong side of the line. The line marks the stud’s edge; the X shows which side the 1½″ of stud occupies. Be consistent along the whole plate.

On center (OC) spacing measures from the center of one stud to the center of the next: usually 16″ (walls, floors) or 24″. Sheets of plywood, OSB and drywall are 48″ wide, so with 16″ or 24″ layout their edges fall on the center of a stud, where they can be nailed.

The trick: hook the tape on the end of the plate and make the first line at 15¼″, with the X past it. That puts the stud’s center at 16″. Every following line is 16″ further (31¼, 47¼…), which is why tapes print the 16″ marks in red.

Layout lines from the end of the plate
Formula
layout line n = n × OC − ¾″
studs ≈ wall length ÷ OC, rounded up, + 1

Plus extra studs at corners, intersections and openings.

Worked example

A 12-foot (144″) wall at 16″ OC.

  1. Lines at 15¼, 31¼, 47¼, 63¼, 79¼, 95¼, 111¼, 127¼. End stud flush at 142½–144.
  2. Studs: 144 ÷ 16 = 9, + 1 = 10 (the first at the end, eight on layout, one at the far end).
Common mistake

Marking the first stud at 16″. Then its center is at 16¾″ and every sheet edge misses the studs by ¾″, leaving nothing to nail to.

A stud wall is a ladder of vertical studs between a bottom plate (sole plate) and a doubled top plate. Openings need a few special pieces:

  • Header: a beam over the opening that carries the load above it.
  • King stud: full-height stud on each side of the opening.
  • Jack stud (trimmer): shorter stud nailed to the king that holds up the header.
  • Sill: the horizontal piece at the bottom of a window opening.
  • Cripples: short studs above the header and below the sill, on layout.
Worked example

Count the pieces for one window in a 16″ OC wall: 2 kings, 2 jacks, 1 header, 1 sill, cripples above and below wherever a layout mark falls inside the opening.

Common mistake

Leaving out the jack studs and nailing the header to the kings. Nails alone won’t carry the load; the header must bear on jacks.

Wall height is the stud length plus the plates. For 8-foot ceilings, lumber yards sell precut studs at 92⅝″: add a 1½″ bottom plate and a doubled 1½″ top plate and the wall is 97⅛″, which leaves room for 96″ drywall sheets plus the ceiling drywall.

Formula
wall height = stud + 1½″ × number of plates
Worked example
  1. 8-foot ceiling: 92⅝ + 3 × 1½ = 97⅛″
  2. 9-foot ceiling with 104⅝″ precuts: 104⅝ + 4½ = 109⅛″
  3. A custom 100″ wall: stud = 100 − 4½ = 95½″
Common mistake

Cutting studs to 96″ for an “8-foot wall”. The wall ends up 100½″ tall and the drywall comes up short at the top.

A rough opening (RO) is the framed hole a door or window goes into, a little bigger than the unit so it can be shimmed plumb. The manufacturer gives the RO; for a typical prehung interior door it is the door width + 2″ by 82½″ tall.

The header sits on the two jack studs, so it is 3″ longer than the opening (two 1½″ jacks). Its depth (2×8, 2×10, doubled or not) depends on the span and the load above it: take it from your plans or the lintel span tables in Part 9 of the Alberta building code (the code calls headers lintels).

Formula
header length = RO width + 3″
jack length = RO height − bottom plate (1½″)

For doors the bottom plate is cut out of the opening after the wall is up.

Worked example

32″ door, RO 34″ × 82½″.

  1. Header: 34 + 3 = 37″
  2. Jacks: 82½ − 1½ = 81″ each
  3. Kings: full 92⅝″ studs, nailed to the jacks.
Common mistake

Measuring the RO from the top of the bottom plate. Door RO heights are measured from the subfloor; the plate is later cut out of the doorway.

Walls are usually framed lying flat on the floor. Before sheathing, square the frame by making its diagonals equal (), then tack it to the floor or nail on a temporary diagonal brace so it can’t rack while you sheathe and stand it up.

Worked example

144″ × 97⅛″ wall.

  1. Target diagonal: √(144² + 97.125²) = 173.7″
  2. Diagonals read 174⅛″ and 173¼″: push the long diagonal’s corners together until both read about 173¾″.
Common mistake

Sheathing first and squaring later. The sheathing locks in whatever shape the frame had. Square, then sheathe.

A simple gable roof has pairs of common rafters leaning against a ridge board and resting on the wall’s top plates.

  • Span: outside of wall to outside of wall.
  • Run: the horizontal distance one rafter covers, half the span for a symmetric gable.
  • Rise: how far it climbs over its run. Pitch is rise per 12″ of run ().
  • Overhang: the part of the rafter (the tail) beyond the wall.
Worked example

24′ span at 6-in-12.

  1. Run: 24 ÷ 2 = 12′
  2. Rise: 12 × 6 ÷ 12 = 6′ (to the ridge’s center line, at the outside of the wall)
Common mistake

Using the inside wall-to-wall distance as the span. Span is outside to outside, since the rafter’s bird’s mouth sits at the outside edge of the plate.

The rafter runs along the slope, so its length is the run times the slope factor (). One correction: the rafters stop against the ridge board, not at its center line, so each run is shorter by half the ridge thickness. This is the ridge reduction.

The resulting line length runs from the ridge plumb cut to the plumb line at the outside of the wall. Measure it between those two plumb lines along any line parallel to the rafter’s edge, such as the top edge.

Formula
run = span ÷ 2 − ridge thickness ÷ 2
line length = run × √(1 + (rise ÷ 12)²)
Rafter quick table
Worked example

24′ span, 6-in-12, 1½″ ridge board.

  1. Run: 144 − 0.75 = 143¼″
  2. Line length: 143.25 × 1.118 = 160.16″ ≈ 160³⁄₁₆″ (13′ 4³⁄₁₆″)
  3. Plumb cut at the ridge: 26.6° on the saw.
Common mistake

Forgetting the ridge reduction. Each rafter comes out ¾″ × 1.118 ≈ ⅞″ too long and the ridge ends up higher than planned.

The bird’s mouth is the notch that lets a rafter sit flat on the top plate. It has two cuts: a level seat cut that bears on the plate, and a plumb heel cut that sits against the outside of the wall.

The seat is usually as wide as the wall (3½″ on a 2×4 wall). Don’t notch too deep. The building code limits notches in roof framing to one-third of the member’s depth, and the usual rule for a bird’s mouth matches: leave at least two-thirds of the rafter’s depth above the seat, and never cut past the plate.

Formula
heel height (plumb) = seat length × rise ÷ 12
Worked example

2×6 rafter, 6-in-12, 3½″ seat.

  1. Heel: 3.5 × 6 ÷ 12 = 1¾″
  2. Depth removed, measured square to the rafter: 1.75 × cos 26.57° = 1.57″, about 28% of 5½″.

Under a third of the depth, within the one-third limit. Your plans or the City may set other limits for engineered roofs.

Common mistake

Overcutting the corner of the notch with a circular saw (the round blade cuts further on the bottom than the top). Stop at the line and finish the corner with a hand saw.

See also

The rafter tail extends past the wall to form the eave. Its length along the rafter is the overhang’s horizontal run times the slope factor. The tail usually ends in a plumb cut (so a fascia board sits vertical) and sometimes a level cut underneath (to carry a flat soffit).

Formula
tail length = overhang run × slope factor
Worked example

12″ overhang at 6-in-12.

  1. Tail: 12 × 1.118 = 13.42″ ≈ 13⁷⁄₁₆″ beyond the heel plumb line.
  2. Tail plumb cut: 26.6° on the saw.
Common mistake

Cutting tails before the rafters are installed. Many framers leave tails long, then snap a line across all of them and cut them in place so the fascia runs dead straight.

The speed square has a COMMON scale marked in rise per 12 (the same arc as the degree scale). To mark a plumb cut for any pitch: hold the pivot on the top edge of the rafter, swing the square until the pitch number on the COMMON scale lines up with that same edge, and draw along the pivot leg. That line is plumb when the rafter is in place.

A level line (for a seat or level cut) is square to the plumb line: slide the square along the plumb line and mark across it.

Worked example
  1. Ridge end: pivot on the top edge at the rafter’s end, swing to COMMON 6, mark the plumb line.
  2. Measure the line length (160³⁄₁₆″) along the top edge, mark, and draw a second plumb line there: the heel line.
  3. Mark the 3½″ seat square off the heel line.
Common mistake

Reading the COMMON scale on the degree row. On most squares 6 on the COMMON row is 26.6°; 6 on the degree row is a very different line.

See also

The traditional layout with a framing square: set it on the rafter with 12 on the blade and the rise (6) on the tongue, both touching the top edge. Draw along the tongue for the plumb line, then step the square along the rafter, one step for each foot of run. A leftover part-foot is one partial step using that many inches on the blade.

Worked example

Run 143¼″ = 11 feet and 11¼″.

  1. From the ridge plumb line, make 11 full steps at 12 and 6.
  2. One partial step: slide the square until the blade reads 11¼″ at the last mark, keeping the same angle (tongue at 5⅝″, half of 11¼).
  3. The tongue’s line there is the heel plumb line.
Common mistake

Letting the square creep between steps. Use stair gauges clamped at 12 and 6 so each step lands exactly where the last one ended.

On a hip roof the corners slope too. The hip rafter runs diagonally, at 45° in plan, from the building’s corner up to the ridge; a valley rafter runs along an inside corner where two roofs meet. Because the hip runs diagonally, it covers 16.97″ of run (√2 × 12) for every 12″ that the common rafters cover, while rising the same amount. So its pitch is shallower: “6 in 16.97” for a 6-in-12 roof.

Hips also need cheek cuts at the top: two 45° bevels that let them nest against the ridge and commons. This is advanced layout; this page is only the idea.

Formula
hip slope = arctan(rise ÷ 16.97)
hip length per foot of common run = √(16.97² + rise²)
Worked example

6-in-12 main roof.

  1. Hip slope: arctan(6 ÷ 16.97) = 19.47°
  2. Hip per foot of common run: √(16.97² + 6²) = 18.0″
Common mistake

Cutting the hip’s plumb cuts at the common rafter’s angle. The hip is shallower, so its plumb cut is 19.5°, not 26.6°.

A stair climbs a total rise (floor to floor) in equal steps. Each step has a riser (the vertical height) and a tread (the horizontal depth you step on); the sloping boards that carry them are stringers. The front edge of a tread that overhangs the riser below is the nosing.

Every riser in a flight must be the same height, because your feet expect it. Limits for stairs in a house (the code’s private stairs) under the Alberta building code:

  • Rise: 125–200 mm per step (about 5″ to 7⅞″).
  • Run: 255–355 mm (about 10¹⁄₁₆″ to 14″), nosing to nosing. Each tread’s depth is from the run to the run + 25 mm (1″).
  • Uniformity: neighbouring risers or runs within 5 mm (³⁄₁₆″); tallest and shortest in a flight within 10 mm (⅜″).
  • Headroom: 1950 mm (6′4⅞″) minimum.
  • Width: 860 mm (33⅞″) minimum.
  • Handrail: 865–965 mm (34″–38″) above the nosings; exterior stairs with more than 3 risers need at least one.

A comfort check used by many builders: 2 × riser + tread ≈ 24–25″.

Private-stair limits from Part 9 of the National Building Code – 2023 Alberta Edition. Stairs serving more than one dwelling or a public building have stricter limits. Check with the City of Calgary.

Common mistake

Making the top or bottom step a different height to “use up” leftover rise. It is a classic trip hazard and fails inspection. Divide the total rise evenly.

Start from the total rise, measured from the finished surface below to the finished surface above. Divide by a comfortable target riser (about 7½″) and round up to get the number of risers. Divide the total rise by that whole number for the exact riser height. There is one fewer tread than risers, because the top “tread” is the floor or deck you arrive on.

Formula
risers = total rise ÷ 7½, rounded up
riser = total rise ÷ risers
treads = risers − 1 · total run = treads × tread
Quick stair table (target riser 7½″, max 200 mm ≈ 7⅞″, 10½″ runs)
Worked example

A deck 42″ above the patio, 10½″ treads.

  1. Risers: 42 ÷ 7.5 = 5.6 → 6
  2. Riser height: 42 ÷ 6 = 7″
  3. Treads: 5; total run 5 × 10½ = 52½″
  4. Comfort: 2 × 7 + 10½ = 24½″ ✓
  5. Code check: 7″ = 178 mm (≤ 200 ✓); 10½″ = 267 mm (≥ 255 ✓).
Common mistake

Measuring total rise to the ground right under the deck edge, then building the stair to land 4½ feet out where the ground is lower. Measure the rise at the spot where the bottom step will land (a level and a straight board help).

Stringers are usually cut from 2×12s. Clamp stair gauges (small brass stops) on a framing square at the riser height on the tongue and the tread depth on the blade. Lay the square on the stringer with both gauges against the top edge, trace the riser and tread, slide along to the end of the tread, and repeat. Every step comes out identical.

Worked example

The 42″ deck stair: gauges at 7″ and 10½″.

  1. Lay out 6 risers and 5 treads.
  2. Stringer stock: √(42² + 52.5²) = 67.2″ plus about a foot for the ends: an 8-foot 2×12.
  3. Check: the throat (the narrowest wood left between the notches and the bottom edge) should be at least about 3½″–5″; the City’s deck guide or your plans give the minimum.
Common mistake

Overcutting the notch corners with the circular saw, which weakens the stringer. Stop at the lines and finish with a hand saw or jigsaw.

See also

Every riser is laid out the same height, but the treads add thickness to the steps. At the top, each tread raises its step by its own thickness, and so does the step below it, so the steps between treads stay equal. At the bottom there is no tread under the first step. The first step would therefore be one tread thickness too tall. To fix it, cut one tread thickness off the bottom of the stringer: dropping the stringer.

Worked example

7″ risers with 1½″ (2×6) treads.

  1. Laid out: the first riser is 7″ from the stringer’s bottom cut to the first tread line.
  2. Cut 1½″ off the bottom: the first tread cut sits 5½″ up; with its 1½″ tread it is 7″ above the ground, like every other step.
Common mistake

Forgetting the drop on the bottom, which leaves the first step 1½″ taller than the rest: the step people trip on.

Blocking is short lengths of framing lumber nailed between studs or joists: for fire blocking, backing for cabinets and grab bars, and to stiffen floors. Cut blocks to the clear space between framing, which at regular layout is the spacing minus one stud thickness.

Stagger blocks up and down by their own width so you can nail through the stud into each end.

Formula
block length = OC spacing − 1½″
Worked example
  1. 16″ OC: 16 − 1½ = 14½″
  2. 24″ OC: 22½″
  3. Measure any bay that isn’t on layout (next to openings or corners) and cut that block to fit.
Common mistake

Cutting blocks a hair long and hammering them in. They bow the studs. Cut them exact or ¹⁄₁₆″ short.

Wall sheathing (plywood or OSB, 4′ × 8′) is nailed to the studs to brace the wall. Two rules make it go on right:

  • Every vertical edge lands on a stud center. With 16″ or 24″ layout from the end of the wall, that happens automatically every 48″.
  • Leave about ⅛″ between sheets so they have room to swell.

Sheathe right over window and door openings and cut them out afterwards, following the framing from inside with a saw or router. It is faster and the edges line up exactly.

Worked example

A 12-foot wall at 16″ OC.

  1. Sheet 1: 0–48″, edge on the stud centered at 48″.
  2. Sheet 2: 48⅛–96⅛″; sheet 3 fills the rest, cut to about 47¾″.
Common mistake

Butting sheets tight. When they swell in wet weather, they buckle between studs. Use a 10d nail as a spacer for the ⅛″ gap.

See also

A deck starts with strings, not wood. Fasten the ledger (the board bolted to the house) level, then set up batter boards (small frames on stakes beyond each outside corner) and stretch mason’s strings from them to mark the deck’s outer edges. Square the strings to the house with a 3-4-5 triangle, then confirm with the diagonals.

Worked example

A 16′ × 12′ deck.

  1. Square each side string with 6-8-10 feet off the ledger.
  2. Diagonals: √(16² + 12²) = 20′ each. Adjust the strings until both measure 20′.
  3. Post centers are measured along the strings; drop them to the ground with a plumb bob.
Calgary deck rules
  • A building permit is needed for an uncovered deck more than 0.6 m (2′) above grade at any point, and for rebuilding, extending or adding stairs to one.
  • Guards are required where the walking surface is more than 600 mm (23⅝″) above the ground beside it.
  • Guard height: at least 900 mm (35½″) where the deck is up to 1800 mm (5′11″) above the ground; 1070 mm (42¼″) where it is higher.
  • Openings in guards must stop a 100 mm (4″) ball from passing through.
  • Deck stairs follow the private-stair limits in .
Common mistake

Setting posts before checking the diagonals. A string that is an inch out at the corner puts a post in the wrong hole. Check diagonals, then dig.

Posts are usually set long and cut in place. Carry a level line from the ledger (with a long level on a straight board, a water level or a laser), mark it on one face, and wrap it around all four faces with a speed square. A 4×4 (3½″) is thicker than a 7¼″ circular saw cuts in one pass, so cut from two opposite sides.

For a deck with a corner cut off at 45°, the rim and decking at that corner meet at 135°: each piece takes half, so the saw is at 22.5° ().

Worked example

Posts that carry a doubled 2×10 beam hung below the joists.

  1. Level line from the bottom of the ledger to each post.
  2. Post top = that line − joist depth (9¼″) − beam depth (9¼″), if the beam sits under the joists on top of the posts.
  3. Wrap the line, cut from two sides.
Safety
  • Cutting overhead or on a ladder is where circular-saw accidents happen. Cut posts at a comfortable height, or mark them and cut them on sawhorses before setting.
Common mistake

Marking all posts the same length from the ground. Grade is never level; always transfer height from the ledger.
