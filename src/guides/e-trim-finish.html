Trim is where angled cuts are most visible and walls are least square. This guide covers which way each corner goes, how to measure between corners, coping (the carpenter’s answer to crooked inside corners), the finishing details of baseboard and casing, and crown molding.

Key points

  • Inside corner: long point on the back (wall side). Outside corner: long point on the front.
  • Cope inside corners, miter outside corners.
  • Scarf long runs over a stud with matching bevels; the steeper the bevel, the longer the overlap.
  • Crown nested upside down and backwards: miter only, 90° − corner ÷ 2. Crown flat: use the compound table.

At an inside corner the two pieces of trim meet with their backs reaching all the way into the corner, so the long point is on the back (the wall side) and the front face is shorter. At an outside corner the trim wraps around the wall, so the long point is on the front and the back stops at the wall’s corner.

This is the most common trim mistake: cutting a perfect 45° in the wrong direction. Before cutting, hold the board in place and say “long point on the wall” or “long point in the room.”

Rule
inside corner: front = back − t × tan(saw)
outside corner: front = back + t × tan(saw)

t = trim thickness. At a 90° corner (saw 45°) the front is shorter or longer than the back by exactly t.

Worked example

⅝″-thick baseboard, square corners.

  1. Inside corner: the front stops ⅝″ short of the corner.
  2. Outside corner: the front sticks out ⅝″ past the wall’s corner.
Common mistake

Measuring the front face of a piece that runs between two inside corners. Measure wall to wall; that is the back length, and the saw makes the front shorter on its own.

Measure trim lengths along the wall, at the height the trim will sit, and write down which kind of corner is at each end. The measurement is the length of the back of the trim; the saw produces the front.

  • Inside to inside: wall to wall. Both ends long point at the back.
  • Inside to outside: from the inside corner to the outside corner of the wall. Mark that length on the back; the outside end’s front long point is t longer.
  • Outside to outside: the wall’s length between its two outside corners, on the back.

For outside corners, the most reliable method is to skip the tape: hold the board in place and mark the wall corner on its back edge directly.

Worked example

Wall 96″ from an inside corner to an outside corner; baseboard ⅝″ thick.

  1. Back length: 96″.
  2. Front at the inside end: ⅝″ short of the corner. Front at the outside end: 96⅝″ from the inside corner.
  3. Cut the board a hair long (¹⁄₁₆″), test, and trim.
Common mistake

Measuring at the floor, where the wall flares out with drywall mud. Measure at the height of the top of the baseboard.

A mitered inside corner only closes if the corner is exactly the angle you cut. Real inside corners are rarely 90°, and when they are off, the two faces of the miter pull apart and a gap shows right at the front. Wood movement makes it worse over time.

A coped joint avoids this. The first piece runs square into the corner. The second piece has its end cut to the exact shape of the first piece’s profile, so it slides over the face of the first piece like a jigsaw piece. If the corner is a degree or two off, the coped piece just sits at a slightly different angle, and the joint still looks tight.

Worked example

A 93° corner with 3½″ baseboard.

  1. Mitered at 45° each: each piece is 1.5° off, and the gap at the front grows to about 3.5 × tan 3° ≈ ³⁄₁₆″ across the joint.
  2. Coped: the second piece simply meets the first at 93°; no gap.
Common mistake

Coping outside corners. Coping only works at inside corners, where one piece can slide over the other. Outside corners are always mitered.

See also

Coping takes a few tries to learn and then becomes quicker than fussing with miters. You need a miter saw, a coping saw with a fine blade, and a file or rasp.

  1. Install the first piece square-cut, tight into the corner.
  2. On the second piece, cut a 45° inside miter (long point at the back) as if you were mitering the corner. The line where the cut meets the face is the profile line. Rub a pencil along it so it shows.
  3. With the coping saw, cut along the profile line, tilting the saw about 30° so you remove wood from behind the line (a back-cut). Only the thin front edge should remain at the line.
  4. For the flat top part of the profile, cut straight (square) so the top edge stays crisp.
  5. Test-fit against a scrap of the same molding. File or rasp high spots until it closes.
Worked example

Finding the length of the coped piece.

  1. Measure wall to wall and mark that length on the back of the second piece.
  2. Make the 45° reveal cut with its long point on that mark.
  3. After coping, the front edge lands exactly on the face of the first piece, ⅝″ out from the wall for ⅝″ baseboard.

Cut ¹⁄₁₆″ long, test, and trim to fit.

Common mistake

Coping straight (no back-cut). Then the whole end has to match the first piece exactly, and any bump holds the joint open. Back-cut generously; the front edge does all the work.

With coped inside corners, order matters: each coped piece has to go in after the piece it fits over. A good plan:

  1. Start on the wall opposite the door with a piece square-cut at both ends, tight into both corners.
  2. Work around the room so each new piece is coped on the end that meets the previous piece, and square or mitered at the other end.
  3. Miter outside corners; install mitered pairs together.
  4. Save the least visible joint (often behind the door) for last: the last piece may need coping at both ends.
Worked example

The room in the figure, with the door in the bottom wall.

  1. Piece 1 on the top wall, square at both ends (S–S).
  2. Pieces 2 and 3 on the side walls: coped at the top where they meet piece 1, square into the bottom corners (C–S).
  3. Pieces 4 and 5 on the bottom wall: coped into the corners, square against the door casing (C–S).

Every inside corner has one square piece and one coped piece. An outside corner anywhere along the way would be a mitered pair (M).

Common mistake

Starting beside the door and ending opposite it, where everyone looks first. Put the tricky double-coped piece where it will be least noticed.

When a wall is longer than your trim stock, join two pieces end to end with a scarf joint: both ends cut at the same bevel so they overlap, instead of butting square. A scarf hides the seam better, and when the wood shrinks it shows as a thin line rather than a gap.

Put the joint over a stud so both pieces can be nailed, glue the bevels, and overlap so the seam faces away from the main view (the front piece laps over the back one).

Formula
overlap = t × tan(bevel)

t = trim thickness. A steeper bevel gives more overlap and a stronger, less visible joint.

Worked example

⅝″ baseboard, 45° bevel.

  1. Overlap: 0.625 × tan 45° = ⅝″
  2. Both pieces measured to the long point of their bevel; both land on the stud at 64″ from the corner.
Common mistake

Cutting one bevel at 45° left and the other at 45° left too. The two bevels must be opposite hands (one piece’s bevel faces the wall, the other faces the room) so they lap. Dry-fit before nailing.

Where trim ends in open space (a baseboard stopping at an opening, a chair rail, a window stool), a square end shows the rough end grain and the back of the profile. A mitered return turns the profile back into the wall: miter the end like an outside corner, and glue on a tiny piece with a matching miter and a square back.

Worked example

A ⅝″-thick baseboard return.

  1. Miter the end of the baseboard at 45°, long point on the front.
  2. Miter the end of a long scrap of the same molding the opposite way.
  3. Cut the return off that scrap with a square cut at ⅝″ from the long point (the depth to the wall).
  4. Glue it on; hold with tape until set.
Safety
  • Never try to hold a ⅝″ piece near a miter-saw blade. Cut it off the end of a long board, and let the blade stop before retrieving it (or use a hand saw).
Common mistake

Nailing the return. It splits. Glue it (with tape as a clamp) or use a single pin nail from the back.

Casing is the trim around a door or window. It doesn’t sit flush with the edge of the jamb (the frame the door hangs in); it steps back by a small reveal, usually ³⁄₁₆″. The reveal hides small wobbles in the jamb edge and looks deliberate.

Mark the reveal on the jamb edges with a combination square set to ³⁄₁₆″. Where the side and head reveal lines cross at each top corner is the short point of the miter.

Formula
head SP–SP = opening width + 2 × reveal
head LP–LP = head SP–SP + 2 × casing width
Worked example

Opening 30″ between jambs, ³⁄₁₆″ reveal, 2½″ casing.

  1. Head short points: 30 + 2 × ³⁄₁₆ = 30⅜″
  2. Head long points: 30⅜ + 2 × 2½ = 35⅜″
  3. Legs: measure from the floor to the head reveal line at each side (floors are rarely level), long point at the top.
Common mistake

Measuring one leg and cutting both the same. Floors slope; measure each side separately.

Crown molding bridges the corner between wall and ceiling at an angle, with a hollow behind it. Two flat strips on its back (the flats) rest on the wall and the ceiling. The angle it sits at is the spring angle, and the common kinds are named for it:

  • 52/38 crown: its back makes 52° with the ceiling and 38° with the wall.
  • 45/45 crown: 45° to each.

To check an unknown crown, set it in the corner of a framing square exactly as it sits on the wall and measure the angles.

Worked example

A crown held in a framing square reaches 3½″ down the wall and 2¾″ out along the ceiling.

  1. Angle from the wall: arctan(2.75 ÷ 3.5) = 38.2°
  2. Angle from the ceiling: 90 − 38.2 = 51.8°

It is 52/38 crown: use S = 38° in the flat-cut formula.

Common mistake

Using the 52/38 settings on 45/45 crown. The corners won’t close. Measure the spring angle of every new batch of molding.

The easiest way to cut crown is nested: stand it on the saw upside down and backwards, leaning between the table and the fence at its spring angle. Think of the saw table as the ceiling and the fence as the wall. Because the crown is held just as it sits on the wall (only flipped), a plain miter does the job; no bevel is needed.

A strip clamped to the table (a crown stop) holds the crown at the right angle for every cut.

Formula
miter = 90° − corner ÷ 2 (bevel 0°)

Worked example

A 135° inside corner in a bay.

  1. Miter: 90 − 135 ÷ 2 = 22.5°, bevel 0°.
  2. Swing the same directions as the table, at 22.5° instead of 45°.

Before cutting good stock, cut a pair from two 12″ scraps, label them “L” and “R”, and hold them up in the corner.

Common mistake

Putting the crown on the saw right side up. Nested only works upside down (ceiling edge on the table). Label your crown stop “CEILING EDGE DOWN”.

Crown too wide to stand up against your fence can be cut lying flat on the table, face up. Then the cut is a compound cut: a miter and a bevel together. The settings come from the compound formula in , with the tilt S equal to the crown’s angle from the wall. Many saws have extra detents at 31.6° (miter) and 33.9° (bevel) for exactly this.

Formula
A = 90° − corner ÷ 2, S = crown’s angle from the wall
miter = atan(sin S × tan A), bevel = asin(cos S × sin A)
Crown settings by corner angle
Worked example

52/38 crown (S = 38°) at a 90° corner: A = 45°.

  1. Miter: atan(sin 38° × tan 45°) = atan(0.616) = 31.6°
  2. Bevel: asin(cos 38° × sin 45°) = asin(0.557) = 33.9°
Common mistake

Using the flat settings with the crown nested, or the other way round. Nested: miter only. Flat: miter and bevel. Write the method on the cut list.
