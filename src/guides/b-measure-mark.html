Every cut starts with a measurement and a mark. This guide covers how to hold, read and trust each layout tool, then how to measure the thing you actually need to cut: a corner, a slope, a length to the long point.

  • Speed square (rafter square): a thick triangle with a lip. Hook the lip on the edge; the leg draws a 90° line and the long side draws a 45° line. For other angles, hold the corner marked PIVOT on the edge and swing the square until the edge of the board lines up with the number you want on the degree scale. Most speed-square degree scales read like the saw (0 = square); check yours by drawing the 0 line and comparing it with the 90° leg.
  • Combination square: a steel ruler that slides through a cast head. The head has a 90° face and a 45° face. It only does those two angles, but very accurately, and it doubles as a depth gauge and marking gauge.
  • Sliding bevel (bevel gauge): a handle and a blade locked by a wing nut. It has no scale; it copies an angle from a corner, a drawing or a protractor and carries it to the wood or the saw.
  • Protractor: a half-circle marked 0° to 180°. Put the center mark on your point with the base along the edge and read where your line crosses the scale. It reads geometry (90° = square), so convert before setting the saw.
Common mistake

Letting the tool’s fence lift off the edge while you mark. Press the lip or head firmly against the edge with one hand and draw with the other, pulling the pencil toward you with its point tight against the blade.

  1. Set a sliding bevel (or digital angle finder) into the corner, handle against one wall and blade against the other. Lock it.
  2. Read the angle by laying the bevel on a protractor, or skip numbers and halve it on paper (below).
  3. Saw setting = 90° − corner ÷ 2.
  4. Cut two scrap pieces and hold them in the corner before cutting good wood.
Worked example

The bevel, laid on a protractor, reads 131°.

  1. 90° − 131° ÷ 2 = 90° − 65.5° = 24.5°

Set the saw to 24.5°. Many saws let you override the 22.5° click-stop and lock anywhere in between.

Common mistake

Measuring the corner at the floor, where drywall mud and tape build up. Measure at the height the trim will actually sit, and check a second spot along the corner.

Hook the tape on the long point, mark the length on the same edge with a small “V” (a crow’s foot) whose tip is exactly on the number, and put an X on the waste side. Then line the blade up so its teeth just touch the tip of the V from the waste side. The kerf then comes out of the waste, and the keeper is exactly the length you marked.

Worked example

Four 31″ frame pieces cut with the blade centered on the line (⅛″ kerf):

  1. Each second cut shortens its piece by half a kerf: 31″ − ¹⁄₁₆″ = 30¹⁵⁄₁₆″

On a square frame that is only ¹⁄₁₆″, but if two opposite sides come out different lengths, the frame won’t close square.

Common mistake

Cutting pairs to length separately. Clamp opposite sides together (or use a stop block, a block clamped to the saw fence at the right distance) so pairs come out identical, even if they are a hair off the plan.
