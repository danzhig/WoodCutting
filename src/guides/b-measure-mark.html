Every cut starts with a measurement and a mark. The first sections cover how to hold, read and trust each layout tool. The last two cover measuring the thing you actually need to cut (a corner, a slope, a length to the long point) and checking that what you built is square.

Key points

  • A tape’s hook slides on purpose: it corrects for its own thickness on inside and outside measurements.
  • Speed squares and saws read from square (0° = square line); protractors read from the edge (90° = square line).
  • Measure corners on the side the trim wraps around, then use saw = 90° − corner ÷ 2.
  • Any rectangle is square when its two diagonals are equal. 3-4-5 makes a right angle anywhere.
  • Check every tool once: flip a square, flip a level. Half the difference is the error.

The metal hook on the end of a tape measure is loose on purpose. It slides by exactly its own thickness. When you hook it over the end of a board and pull, it slides out; when you push it against a wall for an inside measurement, it slides in. Either way, the measurement starts from the true end. This is called true zero.

If the hook is bent or its rivets are worn, every reading is off by the same amount. For the most accurate work, burn an inch: measure from the 1″ mark instead of the hook, then subtract 1″ from the reading.

Method
burning an inch: length = reading − 1″
Worked example

You hold the 1″ mark on the end of a shelf and read 31⅜″ at the far end.

  1. 31⅜ − 1 = 30⅜″

The shelf is 30⅜″ long, measured without trusting the hook.

Common mistake

“Fixing” the loose hook by bending or tightening it. That makes every measurement wrong by the hook’s thickness. If two tapes disagree, compare them both against a steel rule, and use one tape for the whole project.

A folding rule is rigid, so it measures inside spaces (between walls, inside a cabinet) without the sag or hook of a tape. Many have a brass extension slide for exact inside readings.

A story stick skips numbers altogether. You hold a straight stick against the real thing and mark the positions on the stick itself: shelf heights, the rise of each stair, where studs fall. Then you hold the stick against the new part and transfer the marks. No reading means no misreading.

  1. Cut a straight, clean stick a little longer than the part.
  2. Hold it tight against the reference and mark each position with a sharp pencil and a label (“S1”, “S2”).
  3. Hold it against the new part with the same end registered, and transfer each mark.
Worked example

Four shelves in a bookcase, with two sides that must match.

  1. Mark all four shelf heights on the stick from the first side.
  2. Transfer to the second side, with the stick’s bottom on the floor line both times.

Every shelf is level across the case, even if the plan’s numbers were rounded.

Common mistake

Registering the stick from different ends on the two parts. Mark “BOTTOM” on the stick and always start from the same reference edge.

The speed square (also called a rafter square) is a thick right triangle, usually 7″ or 12″, with a raised lip along one side. It is the framer’s everyday marking tool and, held against a board, a circular-saw guide.

  • Hook the lip on the board’s edge and the leg that runs across the board marks a square (90°) line.
  • The long side (hypotenuse) marks a 45° line.
  • The corner where the two short legs meet is the pivot. The degree scale runs along the hypotenuse; other scales (“COMMON”, “HIP-VAL”) give roof pitches.
  • Notches along the leg let you hold a pencil at a fixed distance from the edge for marking a line along the board.
Worked example

Marking a square crosscut on a 2×6 at 30″.

  1. Mark 30″ on the edge with a crow’s foot.
  2. Hook the lip on that edge, slide the square until its leg touches the mark.
  3. Draw along the leg across the face.
Common mistake

Letting the lip ride up on the edge (or on a splinter). The line then runs off square. Press the lip down and in with your fingers and pull the pencil toward you.

See also

For angles other than 90° and 45°, hold the pivot corner on the board’s edge at your mark and swing the square until the board’s edge crosses the degree scale at the angle you want. Then draw along the leg that runs from the pivot.

On most speed squares the degree scale reads like a saw: 0° gives a square line, and the number is how far the line leans away from square. Check yours once: pivot to 0 and compare the line with one drawn using the lip. If they match, your scale reads from square.

Worked example

Marking the end of a brace that needs a 40° saw setting ().

  1. Pivot on the edge at your mark.
  2. Swing until the edge reads 40 on the degree scale.
  3. Draw along the pivot leg. Check: the line should look like a cut the saw at 40° would make.
Common mistake

Drawing along the wrong edge of the square after pivoting. Only the leg that runs from the pivot corner gives the angle on the scale. The hypotenuse gives something else.

See also

The framing square (steel square) is a large L: a 24″ × 2″ blade and a 16″ × 1½″ tongue, meeting at the heel. It checks and marks square on big work (wall corners, sheet goods) and, with small clamp-on stair gauges, lays out repeated angles like stair steps and rafter cuts.

Its angle work is done by numbers, not degrees: set one number on the blade and another on the tongue against the edge of a board, and the square makes the angle of that rise and run.

Rule
blade number : tongue number = run : rise

Line up the blade at the run and the tongue at the rise on the same edge; the tongue then marks a plumb line and the blade a level line for that slope.

Worked example

Check a plywood sheet corner: hold the heel in the corner, blade along one edge, tongue along the other.

  1. Both edges touch the square along their full length: the corner is square.
  2. A gap opening toward the tongue’s end shows how far it is out over 16″.
Common mistake

Dropping the square. A framing square that has been dropped on its corner is often no longer square. Check it with the flip test () before trusting it.

See also

The combination square is a steel rule that slides through a cast head and locks anywhere along it. The head has a 90° face and a 45° face. It is more accurate than a speed square and does four jobs: square lines, 45° lines, depth measurement (lock the rule sticking out of the head), and marking a line parallel to an edge (hold a pencil at the end of the rule and slide the head along).

Any square can be checked with the flip test: draw a line along the blade, flip the square over and draw again from the same point. If the two lines don’t coincide, the square is off by half the gap.

Flip test
square’s error = gap between the two lines ÷ 2
Worked example

On the flip test, the two lines are ¹⁄₃₂″ apart at 6″ from the edge.

  1. Error: ¹⁄₃₂ ÷ 2 = ¹⁄₆₄″ over 6″
  2. As an angle: arctan(0.0156 ÷ 6) ≈ 0.15°

Fine for framing, not for furniture. Many squares can be trued by filing or tapping the head; otherwise, replace it.

Common mistake

Checking a square against another square. If both are off the same way, they agree and fool you. The flip test needs only a straight edge and a pencil.

See also

A protractor is the half-circle marked 0° to 180°. Put its center mark on the corner of the angle, line its base up with one side, and read where the other side crosses the scale.

Most protractors have two scales running in opposite directions. Use the scale that reads 0 on the side your baseline runs. A protractor reads geometry: a square line reads 90°, so convert before setting a saw ().

Conversion
saw setting = 90° − protractor reading

For an angle measured from the board’s edge to the cut line.

Worked example

A drawing shows a brace end at 62° from the edge.

  1. Protractor on the drawing: 62° (from the edge side).
  2. Saw: 90 − 62 = 28°
Common mistake

Reading the wrong scale and getting 118° instead of 62°. Ask whether the angle you are measuring is sharper or blunter than a square corner, and pick the number that matches.

See also

A protractor angle finder is two straight arms hinged at a round scale. Open the arms into a corner, lock them, and read the angle between them. It measures real corners in place, where a flat protractor can’t reach.

Many trim models print a second scale that shows the miter-saw setting directly (half the corner, converted to the saw scale). Read the label next to the scale so you know which number you are looking at.

Worked example
  1. Arms against both walls: the scale reads 135°.
  2. Saw: 90 − 135 ÷ 2 = 22.5°
  3. The miter scale on the tool (if it has one) should read 22.5.
Common mistake

Pressing the arms into the drywall tape and mud bulge right at the corner. Hold them a few inches out along each wall, where the wall is flat.

See also

The sliding T-bevel (bevel gauge) is a handle and a thin blade joined by a wing nut. It has no scale. It copies an angle from one place and carries it to another: from a corner to the wood, from a drawing to the saw, or from one leg to its three siblings.

Because nothing is read, nothing is misread. When you need a number, lay the locked bevel on a protractor.

  1. Loosen the wing nut, set the handle against one surface and the blade against the other, lock.
  2. On the wood: handle against the edge, draw along the blade.
  3. On a miter saw (unplugged): handle against the fence, swing the saw until the blade lies flat along the bevel’s blade.
Worked example

Four chair legs need the same splay angle as a sample leg.

  1. Set the bevel from the sample leg and lock it.
  2. Mark or check all four legs from the same bevel setting.

All four match the sample, and you never needed to know the number.

Common mistake

Knocking the blade while carrying the bevel. Tighten the wing nut firmly, and re-check the setting on the original if the bevel has been set down with other tools.

See also

A digital angle finder looks like the two-arm finder but shows the angle on a screen, often to 0.1°. Many have a hold button to freeze the reading when you can’t see the screen.

A digital angle gauge is a small magnetic cube that measures tilt relative to whatever you zeroed it on. Set it on the saw table, press zero, then stick it on the blade: it reads the blade’s tilt directly. It is the easiest way to set an exact bevel.

  1. Angle gauge: raise the blade fully, set the gauge on the table, press zero.
  2. Stick it on the blade body (not the teeth) and tilt the blade until the reading matches.
  3. Lock the tilt, then re-check: locking can shift it a few tenths.
Worked example

Setting a 43.1° bevel for the splayed planter in .

  1. Zero on the table. Stick to the blade. Tilt until it reads 43.1.
  2. Lock and re-read: 43.2. Nudge back and lock again.

A saw’s bevel scale can’t be read to a tenth; the gauge can.

Common mistake

Zeroing on the floor or a bench instead of the saw table. The gauge measures relative to wherever it was zeroed, so zero it on the surface the cut is measured from.

A level tells you whether a surface is horizontal (level) or vertical (plumb). The bubble sits between the lines when it is. The longer the level, the more it averages out small bumps, so use a 4′ level for walls and a 2′ level for shelves.

Check a level the same way as a square: read it, turn it end for end on the same spot, and read again. If the bubble moves, the level is off, and the true level is halfway between the two readings.

Rule of thumb
out of level over L = L × (rise per 12) ÷ 12

Lift the low end until the bubble centers; the gap under it is how far out the surface is over the level’s length.

Worked example

On a shelf, lifting one end of a 24″ level by ⅛″ centers the bubble.

  1. The shelf is ⅛″ out over 24″, so ¼″ over a 48″ shelf.
Common mistake

Checking a long wall with a short level held in one spot. Slide it along, or use a long level on a straight board, so a local bump doesn’t hide a lean.

A plumb bob is a pointed weight on a string. Gravity makes the string perfectly vertical, so it transfers a point straight down (from a ceiling mark to the floor) or checks a post for plumb over its whole height.

A laser level projects a level or plumb line across a room. It does the same job faster over long distances, but check it with a flip test occasionally, just like a spirit level.

Worked example

Placing a bottom plate directly under a top plate that is already nailed to the ceiling joists.

  1. Hang the bob from the plate’s edge at each end. Let it settle.
  2. Mark the floor under the point; snap a chalk line between the marks.

The studs will stand plumb between the plates.

Common mistake

Marking while the bob still swings, or in a draft. Let it settle, or steady it in a bucket of water.

See also

A chalk line is a string wound through powdered chalk. Stretch it tight between two marks and snap it: it leaves a perfectly straight line, as long as you need. Framers use it for plate lines on floors, cut lines on sheet goods and rows of roof shingles.

  1. Hook or hold the line on the first mark; pull it tight over the second.
  2. Lift the string straight up (not sideways) near the middle, a couple of inches.
  3. Let go. For long lines, have someone hold the middle down and snap each half.
Worked example

Ripping a 4×8 sheet to 23½″ wide with a circular saw.

  1. Mark 23½″ from the factory edge at both ends of the sheet.
  2. Snap a line between the marks, then cut on the waste side ().
Common mistake

Snapping sideways or with a slack line, which bows the line. Pull tight and lift straight up.

A contour gauge is a row of thin sliding pins in a holder. Press it against a shape (a molding profile, a door casing, a pipe) and the pins take the shape; lock them and trace the outline onto your board.

Worked example

Fitting a floor board around a door casing.

  1. Press the gauge against the casing at floor level; lock it.
  2. Trace the outline on the board, aligned with where the board will sit. Cut with a jigsaw or coping saw.
Common mistake

Pressing at an angle so the pins slide unevenly. Hold the gauge square to the surface and push straight in.

See also

Scribing copies the bumps of a wall onto a board so it can be cut to fit tight. Put the board in place (a countertop end, a shelf, a cabinet side), set a compass to the widest gap, then run the compass point along the wall with the pencil on the board. The pencil draws the wall’s shape, offset by the gap.

  1. Hold the board level and square in position, touching the wall at its closest point.
  2. Set the compass to the widest gap.
  3. Keep the compass legs horizontal (don’t tilt it) and trace along the wall.
  4. Cut to the line, back-beveling slightly so only the front edge touches.
Worked example

A shelf touches the wall at one point with a ¼″ gap at its widest.

  1. Set the compass to ¼″.
  2. Trace. Where the gap was widest, the line lands right on the board’s end: nothing comes off there. Where the board touched the wall, the line runs ¼″ in.
  3. Cut to the line and slide the shelf ¼″ toward the wall. It now touches all along.

The shelf ends up ¼″ further into the wall than where it first touched, so start with a board ¼″ long if the final length matters.

Common mistake

Tilting the compass as you go, which changes the distance. Keep both legs in a horizontal line the whole way.

Trim wraps around corners two ways. An inside corner points away from you (where two walls meet inside a room). An outside corner points toward you (the corner of a chimney breast or a hallway opening).

Measure the angle on the side the trim wraps around: inside corners from the room side, outside corners with the tool’s arms wrapped around the corner. Both give an angle under 180°, and both use the same saw formula.

Formula
saw = 90° − measured angle ÷ 2
Worked example
  1. Inside corner reads 91°: 90 − 45.5 = 44.5°
  2. Outside corner reads 88°: 90 − 44 = 46°

Neither is 45°. Walls that are 1–2° out are normal; see for which way the long point goes.

Common mistake

Measuring an outside corner from the room side and getting 270°, then halving that. Wrap the arms around the corner so they read the wall’s own angle.

  1. Set a sliding bevel (or digital angle finder) into the corner, handle against one wall and blade against the other. Lock it.
  2. Read the angle by laying the bevel on a protractor, or skip numbers and halve it on paper (below).
  3. Saw setting = 90° − corner ÷ 2.
  4. Cut two scrap pieces and hold them in the corner before cutting good wood.
Worked example

The bevel, laid on a protractor, reads 131°.

  1. 90° − 131° ÷ 2 = 90° − 65.5° = 24.5°

Set the saw to 24.5°. Many saws let you override the 22.5° click-stop and lock anywhere in between.

Common mistake

Measuring the corner at the floor, where drywall mud and tape build up. Measure at the height the trim will actually sit, and check a second spot along the corner.

See also

To measure a slope (a sloped ceiling, a roof, a ramp, the line of existing stairs) you want either its pitch (rise per 12″) or its angle. Two quick ways:

  • Level and tape: hold a level horizontal with one end on the slope, and measure straight down from the level’s 12″ mark to the slope. That drop is the rise per 12.
  • Digital angle finder: lay it on the slope and read degrees directly.
Formula
angle = arctan(drop at 12″ ÷ 12)
Worked example

The drop at 12″ is 3½″.

  1. Pitch: 3½-in-12.
  2. Angle: arctan(3.5 ÷ 12) = 16.26°

A board that must sit plumb against this slope gets a 16.3° plumb cut ().

Common mistake

Measuring over 6″ and forgetting to double it. If you measure over any length other than 12″, scale it: rise per 12 = drop × 12 ÷ length.

See also

Hook the tape on the long point, mark the length on the same edge with a small “V” (a crow’s foot) whose tip is exactly on the number, and put an X on the waste side. Then line the blade up so its teeth just touch the tip of the V from the waste side. The kerf then comes out of the waste, and the keeper is exactly the length you marked.

Worked example

Four 31″ frame pieces cut with the blade centered on the line (⅛″ kerf):

  1. Each second cut shortens its piece by half a kerf: 31″ − ¹⁄₁₆″ = 30¹⁵⁄₁₆″

On a square frame that is only ¹⁄₁₆″, but if two opposite sides come out different lengths, the frame won’t close square.

Common mistake

Cutting pairs to length separately. Clamp opposite sides together (or use a stop block, a block clamped to the saw fence at the right distance) so pairs come out identical, even if they are a hair off the plan.

A four-sided shape with equal opposite sides is a rectangle (square corners) exactly when its two diagonals are equal. This is the fastest way to check a frame, a cabinet, a wall or a deck, because you only need a tape.

If one diagonal is longer, push the two corners at the ends of the long diagonal toward each other until both diagonals match.

Formula
diagonal of a true rectangle = √(width² + height²)
fix: push the long diagonal’s corners by half the difference
Worked example

A 48″ × 30″ frame. Diagonals measure 56¾″ and 56½″.

  1. Target: √(48² + 30²) = 56.60″ ≈ 56⅝″
  2. Difference: ¼″. Push the corners of the 56¾″ diagonal together by about ⅛″.

Both diagonals now read 56⅝″ and every corner is square.

Common mistake

Checking diagonals before the opposite sides are equal. Diagonals only prove square on a shape whose opposite sides already match.

See also

A triangle whose sides are 3, 4 and 5 units long always has a perfect right angle between the 3 and 4 sides. That lets you lay out a square corner of any size with just a tape: measure 3 along one line, 4 along the other, and adjust until the diagonal between the marks is exactly 5.

Scale it up for accuracy on big layouts: 6-8-10, 9-12-15 or 12-16-20 (feet or any unit).

Formula
3² + 4² = 5² (9 + 16 = 25)
3-4-5 scaled up
Worked example

Squaring a wall layout off an existing wall.

  1. Mark 6′ along the existing wall from the corner.
  2. Swing a tape 8′ from the corner along the new line.
  3. Move the new line until the diagonal between the marks is 10′ exactly.
Common mistake

Using a small 3-4-5 (in feet) for a 30-foot wall. A ⅛″ error over 3′ becomes 1¼″ over 30′. Use the biggest triangle that fits.
