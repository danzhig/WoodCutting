This is the math of angled cuts, always with the intuition first and a 2×4 as the running example. Every formula here is also on the page and in the .

Key points

  • Two pieces meeting at a corner each take half of it: saw = 90° − corner ÷ 2.
  • Polygon frames: saw = 180° ÷ number of sides.
  • Saw scales read from square (0° = square cut); geometry reads from the edge (90° = square cut).
  • Long point moves by W × tan(saw angle). At 45° it moves one full width.
  • Compound cuts: miter = atan(sin S · tan A), bevel = asin(cos S · sin A).

When two boards meet at a corner and both are cut, the joint line runs exactly through the middle of the corner. The fancy word is bisector: the line that splits an angle into two equal halves. Because the joint is shared, each board takes half of the corner.

Intuition: fold a sheet of paper so one edge lies on the other. The crease is the bisector, and it is where the two boards touch.

Formula
angle between cut and edge = C ÷ 2
saw setting = 90° − C ÷ 2

C is the corner angle: the angle between the two boards, measured on the side where they touch. The second line converts to a miter-saw reading, explained in .

Worked example

A square picture frame has four 90° corners.

  1. Angle between cut and edge: 90° ÷ 2 = 45°
  2. Saw setting: 90° − 45° = 45°

Every end gets a 45° miter. At 90° the two numbers happen to agree, which is why the saw’s different scale goes unnoticed until you try another corner.

Common mistake

Cutting one piece at the full corner angle and leaving the other square. That only works for a butt joint where one board lands on the face of another (see ). For a corner where both ends show, split the angle.

A regular polygon (a shape with n equal sides) has n equal corners. Walking around its outline you turn a full 360° in total, so each corner turns 360° ÷ n. Each corner is shared by two cuts, so each cut takes half of that turn: 180° ÷ n. That is the saw setting directly.

Formula
saw setting = 180° ÷ n
corner angle C = 180° − 360° ÷ n

n = number of sides. Check: 90° − C ÷ 2 gives the same saw setting.

Miter settings for polygon frames
Worked example

An octagonal mirror frame.

  1. n = 8
  2. Saw setting: 180° ÷ 8 = 22.5° (most miter saws have a click-stop, called a detent, right at 22.5°).
  3. Corner angle: 180° − 360° ÷ 8 = 135°. Each cut makes 67.5° with the edge.

Cut 16 ends (both ends of 8 pieces) at 22.5°, flipping the board between cuts so the long points land on the same edge.

Common mistake

Triangle frames need a 60° setting, and many miter saws stop at 45°–50°. Check your saw’s maximum before you buy wood. Beyond it, mark the line with a protractor and cut it with a handsaw, or use a jig that holds the board at an angle to the fence.

Real walls are rarely perfect. A bay window may have 135° corners, a hexagonal room 120°, and an ordinary “square” corner can easily be 91° or 92°. The half-angle rule still works: measure the real corner, halve it, convert to a saw setting.

Measure on the side where the boards will sit. For baseboard in a room, that is the room side of the walls. A sliding bevel or a digital angle finder does this well (see ).

Formula
saw setting = 90° − (measured corner ÷ 2)
Worked example
  1. Bay corner measures 135°: 90° − 135° ÷ 2 = 90° − 67.5° = 22.5°
  2. Hexagonal room corner 120°: 90° − 60° = 30°
  3. A “square” corner that measures 92°: 90° − 46° = 44°

The out-of-square wall needs 44°, not 45°. Cutting at 45° would leave a gap you can see.

Common mistake

Assuming a room corner is 90° because it looks square. A 2° error splits into 1° per piece, which opens about ¹⁄₁₆″ on a 3½″ board. Measure every corner; it takes seconds.

Not every frame is a regular polygon. A window seat in a bay, a countertop edge along crooked walls, or a frame around an odd-shaped mirror may have every corner at a different angle. The half-angle rule still works; you just apply it one joint at a time.

Check your measurements with one fact: the inside corners of any closed four-sided shape add up to 360° (five sides: 540°; in general (n − 2) × 180°). If yours don’t, one reading is off.

Formula
saw at each joint = 90° − (that corner ÷ 2)
check: sum of corners = (n − 2) × 180°
Worked example

Corners measured at 80°, 100°, 95° and 85°.

  1. Check: 80 + 100 + 95 + 85 = 360° ✓
  2. Joint 1: 90 − 40 = 50°; joint 2: 90 − 50 = 40°
  3. Joint 3: 90 − 47.5 = 42.5°; joint 4: 90 − 42.5 = 47.5°

The piece between joints 1 and 2 is cut 50° at one end and 40° at the other. Label each end on the wood (J1, J2…) before cutting, and use W × tan θ₁ + W × tan θ₂ for its length ().

Common mistake

Measuring one corner and assuming its opposite matches. In irregular shapes they rarely do. Measure and label every corner, and write the saw setting on both pieces that meet there.

When a board ends against the face of another board (a T-joint, or a brace landing on a post), only the arriving board gets cut. Its end must lie flat against the surface it meets, so the cut line is parallel to that surface. There is nothing to share, so that board takes the whole angle, not half.

A diagonal brace between a post and a beam is the classic case. It has two ends, one against each member, and its two saw settings always add up to 90° when the post and beam are square to each other.

Formula
end against horizontal surface: saw = 90° − a
end against vertical surface: saw = a

a = the brace’s angle above horizontal. Both ends together: (90° − a) + a = 90°.

Worked example

A brace that rises at 50° from horizontal.

  1. Top end, flat against the underside of the beam: 90° − 50° = 40°
  2. Bottom end, flat against the side of the post: 50°
  3. Check: 40° + 50° = 90° ✓

A 45° brace is the easy special case: 45° at both ends.

Common mistake

Cutting both brace ends at the same setting because they “look alike.” Unless the brace is exactly 45°, one end needs a and the other 90° − a. Hold the brace in place and mark both ends before cutting, or label them “post” and “beam.”

In geometry, a square cut makes a 90° angle with the board’s edge. A miter saw calls that same cut 0°, because its scale counts how far the blade has swung away from square. Bevel scales work the same way: 0° is a vertical blade.

So there are two ways to describe any cut: the geometric angle (between the cut line and the edge of the board) and the saw setting (between the cut line and a square line). They always add up to 90°.

Formula
saw setting = 90° − geometric angle
geometric angle = 90° − saw setting
Converting between the two scales
Worked example

A plan says “cut the end at 70° to the edge.”

  1. 90° − 70° = 20°

Set the miter saw to 20°. Setting it to 70° is impossible on most saws, which is often the hint that you have the wrong scale.

Common mistake

Reading an angle off a protractor (geometry) and dialing that number straight into the saw. Before setting the saw, ask: “Is 0 a square cut here?” If it is, you are on the saw scale.

A saw that is half a degree off looks perfect on a single cut. The trouble is that a frame adds up every cut. An octagon has 16 cut ends, and all their errors pile into the last joint you close.

Formula
total error = (number of cut ends) × (error per cut)
gap at the joint ≈ W × tan(total error)
Worked example
  1. Octagon, 16 cut ends, each 0.5° off: 16 × 0.5° = 8°
  2. Gap across a 3½″ joint: 3.5 × tan 8° = 0.49″, about ½″
  3. Square frame, 8 cut ends, each 0.5° off: 4° → 3.5 × tan 4° = 0.24″, about ¼″

To correct from a dry fit, divide the gap angle by the number of cut ends: an 8° gap on an octagon means each cut is 8° ÷ 16 = 0.5° off.

Test-cut routine
  • Cut two scrap pieces, put them together, and check the corner with a square. Any error you see is double the per-cut error.
  • Gap at the outside tips: the saw angle is too small. Gap at the inside (heel): too large.
  • For polygons, cut a full set of short test pieces and dry-fit the whole ring before cutting good wood.
Common mistake

Trusting the saw’s detents. Click-stops wear and can be off by half a degree from the factory. Check 90° and 45° with a combination square after any bump or blade change.

Look at the end of a mitered board from above. The cut line, the square line, and the edge of the board form a right triangle (a triangle with one 90° corner). The board’s width is one side. The distance you slide along the edge from the short point to the long point is the other side. Call it the offset.

The steeper the saw setting, the longer that slide. The tangent (tan) of an angle is simply the ratio “side across from the angle ÷ side next to it,” so it converts width into offset. Any phone calculator has a tan button; make sure it is in degrees (DEG), not radians.

Formula
offset = W × tan(θ)

W = width of the surface being cut across (3½″ on a 2×4 face). θ = saw setting (measured from square).

Worked example

2×4 face, W = 3.5″:

  1. 22.5°: 3.5 × 0.4142 = 1.45″ ≈ 1⁷⁄₁₆″
  2. 30°: 3.5 × 0.5774 = 2.02″ ≈ 2″
  3. 45°: 3.5 × 1.0000 = 3.50″ = 3½″
  4. 60°: 3.5 × 1.7321 = 6.06″ ≈ 6¹⁄₁₆″

Going from 45° to 60° almost doubles the offset. Offsets grow quickly at steep angles.

Common mistake

Using the geometric angle (60° to the edge) where the formula wants the saw setting (30° from square). That swaps tan 30° for tan 60° and triples the offset. If your answer is bigger than the board is wide at anything under 45°, you’ve used the wrong angle.

A mitered frame piece has two lengths: the outside length (long point to long point) and the inside length (short point to short point). The inside length is what you see around the opening, like the picture or the mirror. Each mitered end adds one offset, so a piece mitered at both ends is two offsets longer on the outside.

Formula
outside (LP–LP) = inside (SP–SP) + 2 × W × tan(θ)
inside = outside − 2 × W × tan(θ)

If the two ends are cut at different angles θ₁ and θ₂ (long points on the same edge), use W × tan θ₁ + W × tan θ₂ instead of 2 × W × tan θ.

Worked example

“I need a square frame whose opening is 24″ × 24″, from 2×4s laid flat.”

  1. Square frame → saw at 45°. Offset: 3.5 × tan 45° = 3.5″
  2. Long point length: 24 + 2 × 3.5 = 31″
  3. The frame’s outside size is also 31″ × 31″.

Cut four pieces at 31″ LP–LP. Second example, a hexagon with 12″ inside sides: offset 3.5 × tan 30° = 2.02″, so each piece is 12 + 4.04 = 16.04″ ≈ 16¹⁄₁₆″ LP–LP.

Common mistake

Cutting pieces to the opening size (24″) as if the corners were square. The frame then comes out 7″ too small inside. Decide first whether your number is an inside or an outside dimension, and write LP or SP next to it.

A bevel slopes through the thickness instead of across the face. The triangle is the same, it just stands on its edge. So the formula is identical; only W changes: use the thickness the blade passes through. For a 2×4 lying flat and beveled, that is 1½″.

Typical use: a four-sided box or planter frame where the corners are beveled so no end grain shows on the outside.

Formula
offset = T × tan(bevel)
outer face = inner face + 2 × T × tan(bevel)

T = thickness being cut through (1½″ for a 2×4, ¾″ for a 1×4).

Worked example

A square 2×4 frame (boards standing on edge) that must be 20″ across on the outside, corners beveled at 45°.

  1. Offset: 1.5 × tan 45° = 1.5″
  2. Outer face (long point to long point): 20″
  3. Inner face: 20 − 2 × 1.5 = 17″

An octagonal column wrap at 22.5° bevel on the same 2×4: offset 1.5 × 0.4142 = 0.62″ ≈ ⅝″ per end.

Common mistake

Using the face width (3½″) for a bevel. The blade passes through 1½″ of wood when beveling a flat 2×4, so the offset is much smaller. Look at what the blade actually travels through.

See also

Nominal sizes (what the store calls it) and actual sizes (what you measure) differ. The table uses actual sizes: 2×4 = 1½″ × 3½″, 2×6 = 1½″ × 5½″, 1×4 = ¾″ × 3½″. Face columns are for miters across the face; thickness columns are for bevels.

Offset from short point to long point (decimal inches, then nearest ¹⁄₁₆″)
Common mistake

Using nominal sizes (4″, 6″) in the formula. A 45° miter on a “2×6” moves the long point 5½″, not 6″.

A handful of ratios cover most jobs. Memorise 45° and the “one-sixteenth per degree” rule and you can sanity-check almost any cut list in your head.

Rules of thumb
  • 45°: offset = width. A 3½″ board moves 3½″.
  • 30°: a bit over half the width (× 0.58). 2×4: about 2″.
  • 22.5°: a bit over two-fifths (× 0.41). 2×4: about 1⁷⁄₁₆″.
  • 15°: about a quarter (× 0.27). 2×4: about ¹⁵⁄₁₆″.
  • 60°: about 1¾ widths (× 1.73). 2×4: about 6″.
  • Small angles: each degree adds about W ÷ 57. On a 3½″ face that is almost exactly ¹⁄₁₆″ per degree, and it stays within ¹⁄₃₂″ up to about 20°.
Worked example

A 10° miter on a 2×4 face, no calculator.

  1. 10° × ¹⁄₁₆″ = ⅝″ (exact answer 0.617″)

Close enough to mark. For anything cut on a saw, you don’t need the offset at all: set the angle and measure the long-point length.

Common mistake

Stretching the per-degree rule to steep angles. At 45° it predicts 2.8″ instead of 3.5″. Past about 20°, switch to the named ratios above.

Builders often describe a slope by how much it climbs over a horizontal foot instead of in degrees. The climb is the rise, the horizontal distance is the run, and “6-in-12” pitch means 6″ of rise for every 12″ of run. On roof drawings it is shown as a small triangle with 12 on the flat side.

Pitch is easy to measure with a level and a tape, and it scales: a 6-in-12 roof rises 3″ over 6″ and 5 feet over 10 feet.

Formula
angle = arctan(rise ÷ 12)
rise per 12 = 12 × tan(angle)
sloped length = run × √(1 + (rise ÷ 12)²)

arctan (also written tan⁻¹ or atan) is the calculator button that turns a ratio back into an angle. The square-root factor is the “slope factor.”

Pitch to degrees
Worked example
  1. 6-in-12 to degrees: arctan(6 ÷ 12) = arctan(0.5) = 26.57°
  2. 30° to pitch: 12 × tan 30° = 6.93 → about 7-in-12
  3. Rafter over a 10 ft run at 6-in-12: 10 × √1.25 = 11.18 ft = 11′ 2³⁄₁₆″
Common mistake

Treating pitch as a percentage or as degrees. 6-in-12 is 50% grade and 26.57°, not 6° and not 60%. Always keep the “in-12”.

A board lying on a slope usually needs its end cut plumb (truly vertical, so it looks right to the eye or sits flat against a vertical fascia board) or level (truly horizontal, so it sits flat on something or takes a soffit board). The end of a rafter that sticks out past the wall is the rafter tail.

This is the brace rule from again: a plumb cut lands on something vertical, a level cut on something horizontal.

Formula
plumb cut: saw setting = slope angle
level cut: saw setting = 90° − slope angle
plumb-cut offset = W × rise ÷ 12

The last line is W × tan(slope) written in pitch terms, because tan(slope) is just rise ÷ 12.

Worked example

2×6 rafter (W = 5½″) at 6-in-12:

  1. Slope angle: 26.57°, so the plumb cut is 26.6° on the saw (a hair over the 26.5° many people use).
  2. Plumb-cut offset: 5.5 × 6 ÷ 12 = 2.75″ = 2¾″
  3. Level cut: 90° − 26.57° = 63.4° from square. That is past most saws, so mark it with a square and cut it with a circular saw or handsaw.
Common mistake

Marking the plumb line square to the rafter instead of truly vertical. Lay a speed square or level against the rafter in position, or use the pitch scale on a speed square, so the line is plumb in the finished building.

You need a compound cut when the boards meeting at a corner are tilted, not standing straight up or lying flat. Examples: a planter whose sides lean outward, a tray with sloped sides, a hopper, and crown molding, which sits tilted between wall and ceiling.

The surprise is that neither saw setting equals the angle you see. A four-sided planter still turns 90° at each corner, but because the sides lean, the joint line is no longer a simple 45° across the face. The miter and bevel have to share the job, and how they share it depends on the tilt.

Common mistake

Cutting a splayed box at 45° miter and 15° bevel because “it’s square with a 15° lean.” The corners will not close. Use the formulas below; the settings are 14.5° and 43.1° here.

See also

Two limits give the intuition. If the sides stand straight up (tilt 0°), a square box is cut with a pure 45° bevel. If the sides lie flat (tilt 90°), it is a picture frame with a pure 45° miter. For tilts in between, the miter grows from 0° toward 45° and the bevel shrinks from 45° toward 0°.

Formula
A = 180° ÷ n (or 90° − C ÷ 2 for any corner C)
miter = arctan( sin(S) × tan(A) )
bevel = arcsin( cos(S) × sin(A) )

S = tilt of the sides from vertical. A = the flat-frame saw setting for that corner. Board lies face-down on the saw table. For crown molding, S is the angle between the molding’s back and the wall: 38° for common “52/38” crown, 45° for “45/45” crown.

Worked example

Four-sided planter, sides tilted 15° from vertical.

  1. A = 180° ÷ 4 = 45°
  2. Miter: arctan(sin 15° × tan 45°) = arctan(0.2588 × 1) = 14.51°
  3. Bevel: arcsin(cos 15° × sin 45°) = arcsin(0.9659 × 0.7071) = arcsin(0.6830) = 43.08°

Set the saw to about 14.5° miter and 43° bevel. A 45° tilt gives the well-known pair 35.26° and 30°.

Compound settings (board face-down on the saw)
Common mistake

Mixing up which way the sides are measured. This table uses tilt from vertical. Some charts use the slope from horizontal (90° minus our number). If a chart gives you a bevel above 45° for a square box, it is using the other convention.

A taper is a board or leg that gets gradually narrower along its length, like a table leg that slims toward the floor. It is a long, shallow angled cut running with the grain instead of across it.

Describe a taper by how much wood comes off one face at the narrow end, over what length. From that you get the taper angle (what a jig or saw needs) and the taper per foot (how builders often specify it). Measure the width change in one direction: if only one face slopes in that direction, it loses the whole difference; if both opposite faces slope, each loses half.

Formula
removed per face = (wide − narrow) ÷ faces tapered
taper angle = arctan(removed ÷ taper length)
taper per foot = removed ÷ length × 12

Measure wide and narrow in one direction. “Faces tapered” is 1 if one face slopes in that direction, 2 if both opposite faces do. Table legs are usually tapered on the two inside faces, which are adjacent, so each direction has one sloping face.

Taper angle for material removed from one face
Worked example

Leg 1¾″ square at the top, 1⅜″ square at the floor, tapered on the two inside faces over 24″.

  1. Removed per face (one sloping face in each direction): (1.75 − 1.375) ÷ 1 = 0.375″ = ⅜″
  2. Angle: arctan(0.375 ÷ 24) = 0.90°
  3. Per foot: 0.375 ÷ 24 × 12 = 0.19″ (about ³⁄₁₆″ per foot)

Under one degree. That is why tapers are cut with a jig set by distance, not by reading a saw scale ().

Common mistake

Mixing up “per face” and “total”. A board 4″ wide tapered to 3″ on both edges loses ½″ per edge; take a full 1″ off each edge and it ends up 2″ wide.

See also

Before any jig or saw, draw the taper on the wood. Two measurements define it: where the taper starts (often just below a table apron) and how much comes off at the end. A straight line between those two points is the cut line.

Lay out on the face you will cut, on every leg, with the legs labelled so the tapered faces all end up on the inside of the table.

  1. Mark the start of the taper across the face with a square (here 5″ down, where the apron ends).
  2. At the foot, measure in the amount to remove (⅜″) from the edge that will be cut.
  3. Join the two marks with a straightedge. Hatch the waste side.
  4. Repeat on the second tapered face, which is adjacent to the first on a table leg.
Worked example

A 29″ leg, apron 5″ deep, 24″ tapered, ⅜″ off each inside face.

  1. Start mark: 5″ from the top. Taper length: 29 − 5 = 24″.
  2. Foot mark: ⅜″ in from the inside edge.
  3. After the first face is cut, lay out the second face on the adjacent inside face.
Common mistake

Tapering the outside faces. Mark the top of every leg with its position (front-left, etc.) and an arrow toward the inside corner of the table before laying out.
