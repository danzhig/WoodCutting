A lathe spins the wood while you hold the tool. This guide covers spindle turning: work where the grain runs along the lathe’s axis, such as table legs, tool handles, balusters, pens and candlesticks. (Bowl and faceplate turning, where the grain runs across the axis, use different tools and techniques and are not covered here.)

Key points

  • Face shield on, sleeves and hair secured, spin the work by hand, start slow, and stand out of the line of fire.
  • Speed: diameter (in) × RPM ≈ 6,000–9,000, capped by your lathe and by any vibration.
  • Ride the bevel and cut downhill, from larger diameter to smaller.
  • Size diameters with a parting tool and calipers; a round taper changes radius by half the change in diameter.
  • The spindle roughing gouge is for spindles only; never use it on bowls or faceplate work.
  • Bed (ways): the rails everything slides along.
  • Headstock: the motor end. Its spindle is threaded for chucks and faceplates, and hollow with a Morse taper (MT1 or MT2 on most small and mid-size lathes) that holds the drive center, a pointed center with sharp spurs that grip the wood.
  • Tailstock: slides along the bed and locks. Its quill advances with a handwheel and holds the live center, a point on bearings that spins with the work.
  • Banjo and tool rest: the banjo slides and locks on the bed; the rest sits in it and supports the tool.
  • Speed control (belt steps or a dial) and often an indexing pin for dividing the work into equal parts.

Two numbers describe a lathe: swing (twice the height from the bed to the center, the largest diameter it turns) and the distance between centers (the longest spindle).

Worked example

A lathe with its centers 6″ above the bed and 20″ apart.

  1. Swing: 2 × 6 = 12″
  2. A 29″ table leg won’t fit; it needs a bed extension or a longer lathe.
Common mistake

Ordering accessories without checking the spindle thread and Morse taper size. Both vary between lathes and are printed in the manual.

The lathe’s hazards are different from a saw’s: the danger is being caught by spinning wood, or hit by wood that breaks or is thrown. Most of it is prevented before you switch on.

Before every start
  • Face shield (not just glasses) and dust protection.
  • No loose sleeves, gloves, ties or jewelry; long hair tied back.
  • Check the blank for cracks, loose knots and bark; reject anything suspect.
  • Tailstock and banjo locked; rest about ⅛″–¼″ from the work.
  • Spin the work by hand one full turn to be sure nothing hits the rest.
  • Check the speed setting is low, then stand to the side of the line of fire and switch on.
While turning
  • Tool on the rest before it touches the wood; keep your hands behind the rest.
  • Stop the lathe to move the rest, to measure with a rule, or to inspect.
  • Swing the rest out of the way before sanding.
  • Never reach over or around spinning work.
Common mistake

Switching on a big, square or out-of-balance blank at the speed used for the last small piece. Make “speed down” the last thing you do before you turn the lathe off.

The right speed depends mainly on diameter: the edge of a big blank moves much faster than a small one at the same RPM. A common rule of thumb keeps the product of diameter and speed between about 6,000 and 9,000. For small spindles that gives more than most lathes can do, so the lathe’s maximum becomes the limit.

Start well below the rule for anything square, long and thin, off-center or suspect, and raise the speed only while the lathe runs smoothly. Vibration means too fast.

Rule of thumb
RPM ≈ 6,000 ÷ D to 9,000 ÷ D

D = largest diameter in inches. Never above your lathe’s maximum; lower for roughing, unbalanced or long thin work.

Spindle speeds (capped at 3,500 RPM)
Worked example

Roughing a 3″ square blank, 18″ long.

  1. Its diagonal is about 3 × 1.414 = 4.2″, so treat it as a 4¼″ diameter.
  2. Rule: 6,000 ÷ 4.25 ≈ 1,400 to 9,000 ÷ 4.25 ≈ 2,100 RPM.
  3. Square and long: start around 800–1,000 RPM, raise once it is round.
Common mistake

Using the flat side of a square blank as its diameter. The corners reach out to the diagonal, and they are what hits the rest and your knuckles.

Choose straight-grained wood without cracks or loose knots, cut a little longer than the finished piece (the ends get marked by the centers). Mark the center of each end by drawing the diagonals.

Taking the corners off first, to make an octagon, makes roughing faster, quieter and safer. Rip them off on the table saw with the blade at 45° () or plane them off.

Formula
corner cut for a regular octagon = side × (1 − 1 ÷ √2) ≈ 0.293 × side

Measured along each face from the corner.

Worked example

A 2″ square blank.

  1. Corner cut: 0.293 × 2 = 0.586″ ≈ ⁹⁄₁₆″ along each face.
  2. On the table saw: blade at 45°, fence set so the cut starts ⁹⁄₁₆″ in from each corner. Four passes.
Common mistake

Marking the center by eye. An off-center blank vibrates, wastes wood and leaves flat spots when it is turned round. Always use the diagonals, and punch the center with an awl.

Between centers: the drive center’s spurs bite into the headstock end, the live center into the tailstock end. Seat the spurs (tap the drive center into the end with a mallet off the lathe, or snug the tailstock firmly), then tighten the tailstock until the work turns only with the drive, and lock the quill. Re-snug it after a few minutes of turning, as the centers bed in.

In a chuck: a four-jaw scroll chuck grips a tenon (spigot) turned on one end. This leaves the other end free for work such as a goblet or the end of a handle. The tenon must match the jaws’ shape (often a slight dovetail; check yours), be a little shorter than the jaws are deep, and have a square shoulder that seats flat against the jaws.

Worked example

A tenon for chuck jaws that close fully at 1⅞″ and are ½″ deep.

  1. Diameter: a little over the fully-closed size, about 2″, so the jaws grip near a full circle.
  2. Length: about ⁷⁄₁₆″, just shorter than the jaws, with a square shoulder.
Common mistake

A long tenon that bottoms out in the chuck before the shoulder seats. The work then wobbles and can pull out. The shoulder, not the tenon’s end, must bear on the jaws.

  • Spindle roughing gouge: a wide, deep U. Rounds square stock to a cylinder fast. Spindle work only: its tang is too weak for bowls and faceplate work.
  • Spindle gouge: a shallower flute with a rounded “fingernail” tip. Beads, coves and detail.
  • Skew chisel: a flat blade with its edge at an angle. Planing cuts that leave a glassy surface, V-cuts and beads. Powerful but prone to catches until you learn it.
  • Parting tool: a narrow blade for sizing cuts and for parting work off. The beading and parting tool is a square ⅜″ version that also rolls small beads.
  • Scrapers: flat bars with a burr. Light finishing cuts, held pointing slightly down.
Worked example

Tools for a simple tool handle, in order.

  1. Roughing gouge: square to round.
  2. Parting tool and calipers: size the ferrule and the grip.
  3. Spindle gouge: coves and the end shape. Skew: a clean planing pass.
Common mistake

Using a spindle roughing gouge on a bowl blank. The cut is interrupted by end grain every half turn, and the tang can snap. Use a bowl gouge for bowls.

The tool rest supports the tool so that the wood’s force goes into the rest, not your hands. Set it parallel to the work, about ⅛″–¼″ away from the highest point, at a height where the tool’s edge meets the wood at or slightly above center when the handle is level or a little low.

As the work gets smaller, move the rest in. A rest far from the work gives the tool leverage against you.

Worked example

Roughing a 3″ square blank.

  1. Its corners sweep a 4¼″ circle, so set the rest ¼″ outside that: about 2⅜″ from the center line.
  2. After it is round at 2¾″, stop and move the rest in to ⅛″–¼″ from the new surface.
Common mistake

Adjusting the rest while the lathe runs. Stop it, move the rest, spin by hand, then restart.

A turning tool cuts cleanly when its bevel (the ground face behind the edge) rubs on the wood just behind the cut. The bevel supports the edge and controls the depth, like the sole of a plane. This is called riding the bevel.

The approach: rest the tool with the bevel touching the spinning wood and the edge not yet cutting, then slowly raise the handle until the edge starts to take a shaving. Move along with the bevel still in contact.

Always cut downhill: from a larger diameter toward a smaller one. That way the fibres you cut are supported by wood underneath them, like planing with the grain, and the surface comes out smooth instead of torn.

Worked example

Turning a cove (a hollow).

  1. Start at the top edge of one side and cut down to the bottom center.
  2. Then cut the other side, again from its top edge down to the center. Never cut up the far wall.
Common mistake

Presenting the edge first, without bevel contact. The edge digs in with nothing to control it: a catch, which can tear the work or pull the tool. Bevel first, then lift the handle to cut.

Most spindle shapes are built from a few elements:

  • Bead: a rounded, convex ring.
  • Cove: a hollow, concave ring.
  • Fillet: a short flat step that separates two shapes and makes them read crisply.
  • V-cut: a sharp groove, made with the point of a skew.

Lay out the positions with a pencil held on the rest while the lathe runs slowly, separate the elements with V-cuts or parting cuts, then shape each one downhill.

Worked example

A ¾″ bead on a 1½″ cylinder.

  1. Mark lines ¾″ apart; mark the center line between them.
  2. V-cut each outer line to about ⅛″ deep.
  3. With the spindle gouge, roll from the center line down into each V, one side at a time.
Common mistake

Cutting away the center pencil line. It marks the top of the bead; leave it until the last pass so both halves come out symmetrical.

Diameters on the lathe are set with outside calipers: two curved legs that you set to the target size against a rule, then hold on the work. The standard technique is a sizing cut: a parting tool in one hand pushes straight in while the calipers, in the other hand, ride in the groove. The moment the calipers slip over, you have reached the diameter.

An open-end wrench of the right size makes a quick go/no-go gauge for common tenons.

Worked example

Sizing a 1¼″ neck on a 1¾″ cylinder.

  1. Set the calipers to 1¼″ against a steel rule (lathe off).
  2. Lathe on at a low speed. Parting tool straight in, calipers lightly in the groove.
  3. Stop when the calipers drop over. Depth of cut: (1.75 − 1.25) ÷ 2 = ¼″ per side.
Safety
  • Use calipers with rounded tips, a light touch and low speed. Sharp tips or a firm grip can catch and pull the calipers around. When in doubt, stop the lathe and measure.
Common mistake

Forcing the calipers over. They spring open and read big, so the part comes out undersize. Let them fall over under their own weight.

Turning four matching legs by eye is hard. A story stick () holds every position along the spindle: a thin board with a small notch at each transition, and the diameter needed at each one written beside it. Hold it on the rest against the slowly turning blank, touch a pencil into each notch, and every leg gets the same layout. Then size each position with calipers.

Worked example
  1. Turn the first leg; when you like it, mark its transitions on a stick and caliper each diameter.
  2. For legs 2–4: rough to round, transfer the marks, size every diameter with parting cuts, then connect the sizing cuts.
Common mistake

Registering the stick from different ends. Mark one end “TOP” and always line it up with the top of the blank.

A turned taper changes diameter along its length, but the tool sees only the radius, which changes by half as much. So a round taper’s angle (measured from the center line, the half-angle) uses half the difference in diameters. Compare the flat taper in .

The reliable method is to set sizing cuts along the taper at calculated diameters, then turn away the wood between them down to the bottoms of the cuts, and check with a straightedge.

Formula
half-angle = arctan((D₁ − D₂) ÷ (2 × L))
diameter at x = D₁ − (D₁ − D₂) × x ÷ L
Worked example

1¾″ to 1″ over 20″.

  1. Half-angle: arctan(0.75 ÷ 40) = 1.07°
  2. Sizing cuts at 5″, 10″, 15″: 1⁹⁄₁₆″, 1⅜″, 1³⁄₁₆″
  3. Turn between them down to the bottoms; check with a straightedge laid along the taper.
Common mistake

Using the full diameter difference as if it were one side: that doubles the angle and makes the foot far too thin.

Chair rungs, legs and handles often end in a round tenon that fits a drilled hole. Its diameter matches the hole; its length is a little shorter than the hole is deep, leaving room for glue and a tight shoulder. The shoulder (the step where the tenon starts) should be square or slightly undercut so it closes tight.

Rule
tenon diameter = hole diameter
tenon length = hole depth − about ⅛″
Worked example

A ⅝″ hole drilled 1¼″ deep.

  1. Tenon: ⅝″ diameter (checked with a ⅝″ wrench or calipers), 1¼ − ⅛ = 1⅛″ long.
  2. Undercut the shoulder slightly with the skew’s long point.
Common mistake

Turning the tenon a hair oversize and hammering it in. Dry wood won’t compress much; it splits the part with the hole. Aim for a push fit by hand.

Turning tools dull quickly and cut far better sharp. Most turners sharpen on a slow-speed bench grinder with a fine wheel (friable aluminum oxide or CBN, around 80–180 grit) and jigs that hold the tool at a repeatable angle. Hone lightly afterward if you like.

Safety
  • Face shield at the grinder, tool rest close to the wheel, and never grind on the side of a standard wheel. Check new wheels for cracks before mounting.
Common mistake

Overheating the edge until it turns blue. Light passes, keep the tool moving, and don’t quench high-speed steel in water (it can crack); it doesn’t lose its temper at grinding heat the way carbon steel does.

Sanding is quick on the lathe because the work does the moving. Slow the speed down (about half your turning speed), swing the tool rest out of the way, and sand from below the work with the paper moving so it doesn’t cut rings.

Work through the grits without skipping: 120, 180, 240, then 320 for fine hardwoods. Between grits, stop the lathe and sand by hand along the grain to remove circular scratches. Finishes applied on the lathe include oils, wax and friction polish (burnished on with the lathe running slowly).

Worked example

A 1½″ handle turned at about 3,000 RPM.

  1. Sand at about 1,500 RPM: 120, 180, 240.
  2. Stop after each grit and sand along the grain.
  3. Apply friction polish at about 1,000 RPM with a paper towel pad.
Safety
  • Never wrap cloth or abrasive around your fingers or the work. Use a folded paper towel or pad that will tear free if caught.
Common mistake

Sanding out tool marks with coarse paper. Sanding only follows the shape; fix tearout and ridges with a sharp tool first.
