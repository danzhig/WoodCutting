Each saw has its own scales, its own strengths and its own way of hurting people. For every saw this guide covers the parts and scales, setup and calibration, the main cuts, capacity limits, blade choice, and safety. The table saw gets the longest section because it does the most and demands the most care.

Key points

  • Miter and bevel scales on miter saws read 0° at square. Table-saw miter gauges vary by brand: check yours with a square.
  • Table-saw kickback comes from wood touching the rising teeth at the back of the blade. The riving knife, a straight fence, push sticks and standing out of line prevent it.
  • Never use the table-saw fence as a length stop for crosscuts; use a stop block set before the blade.
  • A circular saw and a jigsaw cut on the upstroke: put the good face down. A table saw and miter saw cut downward: good face up.
  • Check every saw with a test cut: flip one piece and any error shows double.

A miter saw (chop saw) is a circular blade on a hinged arm that you pull down through a board held against a fence. The arm sits on a turntable that swings left and right for miters, and it tilts for bevels. A sliding miter saw also slides forward and back on rails, so it can cut much wider boards.

  • Miter scale: on the front of the turntable, 0° = square cut. Click-stops (detents) usually sit at 0°, 15°, 22.5°, 31.6° (for crown molding), and 45°, both sides.
  • Bevel scale: behind the blade, 0° = blade vertical. Many saws tilt one way only; a dual-bevel saw tilts both ways.
  • Kerf slot: the slot in the table insert shows exactly where the blade exits. Line your mark up with its edge.
Worked example

Setting 24.5° (between detents) for the out-of-square bay corner in .

  1. Squeeze the detent override so the turntable moves freely.
  2. Swing to 24.5° on the scale, lock the miter handle.
  3. Make a test cut on scrap and check it against the corner.
Safety
  • Keep hands out of the marked no-hands zone, usually about 6″ each side of the blade.
  • Wood must be flat on the table and tight to the fence; never cut freehand.
  • Let the blade reach full speed before cutting and stop completely before lifting it.
Common mistake

Locking the miter handle after the turntable has crept off the number. Read the scale again after locking; many saws shift a few tenths when the lock bites.

A miter saw is safe when three things are true: your hands are well away from the blade’s path, the wood can’t move, and the offcut has somewhere to go. Most accidents come from holding short pieces with fingers near the blade, reaching across the blade, or grabbing offcuts while it is still spinning.

Safety rules
  • No-hands zone: about 6″ either side of the blade, and never cross your arms over its path.
  • Clamp any piece too short to hold outside the zone, or cut it from a longer board.
  • Support long boards level with the table at both ends so they don’t tip and pinch the blade.
  • Let the blade stop before lifting it or reaching for an offcut.
  • Unplug before changing blades or adjusting under the guard.
  • Wear eye and hearing protection.
Worked example

You need a 3″-long block with 45° ends.

  1. Don’t cut a 3″ scrap. Miter the end of a 2-foot board first.
  2. Measure 3″, clamp the board, cut the second miter. The block falls free; the hand holding the long board stays a foot away.
Common mistake

Pulling a sliding saw toward you through the wood. Pull the head out first, lower it, then push it back through the cut. Pulling into the wood lets the blade climb toward you.

Every miter saw has a maximum width it can cut, and that width shrinks when you swing the saw. The angled cut line is longer than the board is wide (it is width ÷ cos(miter)), but the blade can only travel so far. A bevel reduces the height the saw can cut through.

Your manual has a capacity chart. As a rough guide, a 10″ non-sliding saw cuts a 2×6 square but only a 2×4 at a 45° miter.

Formula (rough)
width capacity at miter m ≈ capacity at 0° × cos(m)
Worked example

A saw that crosscuts 5½″ at 0°:

  1. At 45°: 5.5 × 0.707 = 3.9″
  2. At 22.5°: 5.5 × 0.924 = 5.1″

A 2×4 (3½″) fits at 45°; a 2×6 (5½″) does not.

Boards wider than capacity
  • For square cuts: cut halfway, flip the board over end-for-end, line the blade up with the first kerf and finish. It needs care to match.
  • Use a circular saw with a speed square or straightedge ().
  • Use a table saw with a crosscut sled ().
Common mistake

Forcing a board that is just too wide. The blade stops short of the back edge and leaves a sliver, and people then try to finish it by lifting the board into the blade. Check the capacity before you start a cut.

The two ends of a frame piece lean opposite ways, and the two pieces meeting at a corner are mirror images. There are two ways to get the second angle:

  • Swing the saw: cut one end with the saw at 45° left, the other at 45° right. The board keeps the same face up and the same edge on the fence. This is the only method for molding with a profile.
  • Flip the board: keep the saw at 45° one way and turn the board over. It saves resetting the saw, but only works for plain square-edged stock.
Worked example

A picture frame from molding with a rabbet on the back.

  1. Face up, rabbet edge against the fence, for every cut.
  2. End 1: saw at 45° right. Measure the LP length. End 2: saw at 45° left.

All four pieces come out identical, with the profile facing the same way.

Common mistake

Flipping profiled molding to get the second angle. The profile ends up upside down on one end, and the corners won’t match. Swing the saw instead.

A stop block clamped to the fence (or a fence extension) at the right distance from the blade makes every piece identical without measuring each one. Set it once from the side of the blade that faces the stop, then just slide each board up to it and cut.

Chamfer or notch the bottom corner of the stop so sawdust doesn’t pack against it and push pieces long.

Worked example

Four frame pieces at 31″ LP–LP.

  1. Miter one end of each board first (saw at 45° right).
  2. Swing to 45° left. Set the stop so a mitered board’s long point touches it with 31″ to the blade.
  3. Cut all four second ends against the stop.
Safety
  • The stop goes on the keeper side. Never trap an offcut between the blade and a stop; it can be thrown.
Common mistake

Measuring to the wrong side of the kerf. Set the stop by measuring to the tooth that leans toward the stop, or better, cut one test piece and measure it.

Detents wear, and saws get knocked. Checking takes five minutes and saves a whole project. Check the blade square to the table (bevel at 0°), square to the fence (miter at 0°), then 45°.

A test cut is more sensitive than a square: cut a wide board, flip one piece over and push the cut edges together. Any gap is twice the saw’s error, because the flip reverses it.

  1. Unplug. Lower the blade and lock it down. Hold a square against the fence and the blade body (not the teeth).
  2. Adjust the miter scale or detent plate per the manual until the square touches evenly.
  3. Test-cut a wide board; flip one half; check the joint. Repeat with two 45° pieces checked against a square.
Worked example

Flipped test pieces from a 5½″-wide board show a ¹⁄₃₂″ gap at one end.

  1. The saw’s error is half: ¹⁄₆₄″ over 5½″.
  2. Angle: arctan(0.0156 ÷ 5.5) ≈ 0.16°.

Small, but on an octagon’s 16 cut ends it adds up to 2.6° (). Adjust it.

Common mistake

Checking against the teeth. Teeth are set wider than the blade body and give a false reading. Put the square on the flat of the blade between teeth.

On a table saw the blade sticks up through a flat table and you push the wood into it. The blade stays put; the wood moves. That makes it the best tool for long straight rips and accurate repeat cuts, and it is why guiding the wood safely matters so much.

  • Rip fence: the long guide parallel to the blade that sets the width of a rip.
  • Miter slots and miter gauge: the gauge slides in a slot to push wood across the blade for crosscuts.
  • Throat plate: the insert around the blade.
  • Riving knife: a curved steel fin just behind the blade that rises and falls with it and keeps the kerf from closing on the blade. Some saws use a fixed splitter instead.
  • Blade guard and anti-kickback pawls: the clear cover over the blade and the toothed fingers that dig in if wood moves backward.
  • Height and tilt wheels: raise the blade and tilt it for bevels.
Worked example

Which part does each cut use?

  1. Ripping a 2×6 down to 3½″: rip fence set 3½″ from the blade.
  2. Cutting a 1×4 to 18″ long: miter gauge or crosscut sled, never the fence.
  3. A 45° bevel rip: tilt wheel, fence on the side the blade tilts away from.
Safety
  • Keep the riving knife installed for every through-cut.
  • Read the next topic, on kickback, before your first cut.
Common mistake

Removing the riving knife “because it gets in the way” and never putting it back. It is the single most effective protection against kickback.

Kickback is when the saw grabs the wood and throws it back at the operator, often hard enough to injure, and sometimes pulls the operator’s hand toward the blade as it goes.

It happens at the back of the blade. Up front, the teeth move down and toward you, pressing the wood onto the table. At the back, the teeth rise up and come toward you. If the wood touches those rising teeth (because the kerf closes, the board twists away from the fence, or an offcut gets trapped), they lift it and fire it forward.

What prevents kickback
  • Riving knife in place, so the kerf can’t close on the rear teeth.
  • Fence parallel to the blade, or the back end toed out slightly (about ¹⁄₆₄″), never toward the blade.
  • Stand to the side of the blade line, never directly behind it.
  • Push the wood all the way past the blade with a push stick, keeping it tight to the fence.
  • Never use the fence as a stop for crosscuts, and never cut freehand.
  • Don’t reach over the blade; let the cut piece go past, turn the saw off, then retrieve it.
Worked example

Ripping a 2×4 that has been drying in the sun. Halfway through, the kerf starts to close behind the blade (the wood had built-up stress).

  1. With a riving knife: the knife holds the kerf open; the cut finishes.
  2. Without one: the pinched wood rides up the rear teeth and kicks back.
Common mistake

Standing directly behind the board to push hard. If it kicks back, it comes straight at you. Stand beside the line and push with a stick.

See also

Three simple things keep your hands far from the blade and the wood under control:

  • Push stick/push block: a notched stick or a handle with a grippy base that pushes the wood the last part of the way. Use one whenever your hand would come within about 6″ of the blade (any rip narrower than about 6″).
  • Featherboard: a board with flexible fingers, clamped to the table or fence, that keeps the wood pressed against the fence. Place it ahead of the blade, never beside or behind it, or it will pinch the kerf.
  • Blade height: set the teeth to clear the top of the wood by about ⅛″–¼″. Less blade above the wood is less blade to touch.
Worked example

Ripping a 1×4 (¾″ thick) to 2½″ wide.

  1. Blade height: ¾ + ¼ = 1″ above the table.
  2. Fence at 2½″. Featherboard on the table before the blade, pressing toward the fence.
  3. Feed by hand until the back end reaches the table edge, then finish with the push stick between the blade and fence.
Safety
  • Keep a push stick on the saw at all times so it is always within reach.
  • Push sticks are cheap and made to be cut. Replace them when chewed up.
Common mistake

A featherboard placed level with or past the blade. It squeezes the kerf shut on the rear teeth, the exact condition that causes kickback.

A table saw is accurate only if three things line up: the blade parallel to the miter slots, the fence parallel to the blade, and the blade square to the table at 0° tilt (and at 45° when tilted). Check them when the saw is new, after moving it, and whenever cuts come out wrong.

  1. Blade to slot: unplug. Mark one tooth. Measure from the slot to that tooth at the front, rotate the blade by hand to bring the tooth to the back, measure again. The two should match within a few thousandths.
  2. Fence to blade: measure from the fence to the same slot at front and back. Parallel, or the back end a hair (≈ ¹⁄₆₄″) further from the blade.
  3. Tilt: raise the blade fully. Zero a digital gauge on the table, stick it on the blade, set 0.0° and 45.0°, and adjust the tilt stops.
Worked example

Slot-to-tooth reads 5.235″ at the front and 5.250″ at the back.

  1. Difference: 0.015″ over the tooth-to-tooth distance (about 8″ on a 10″ blade).
  2. Loosen the trunnion bolts (per the manual) and tap the back of the trunnion toward the slot until the readings match.

Cuts stop burning on one side, and crosscuts come out square.

Common mistake

Aligning the fence to the blade before the blade is aligned to the slot. Always blade first, then fence, then any sleds and jigs that ride in the slot.

A rip on the table saw runs the board along the fence, so the width comes out even along the whole length. The piece between the blade and the fence is normally the keeper.

For thin strips (under about ½″), don’t cut them between the blade and fence, where they can be trapped and thrown. Cut them on the far side of the blade instead, using a thin-rip jig in the miter slot to set the width, and move the fence over for each strip.

Worked example

Ripping a 2×6 (5½″) into two equal pieces with a ⅛″ kerf.

  1. Each piece: (5.5 − 0.125) ÷ 2 = 2.6875″ = 2¹¹⁄₁₆″
  2. Set the fence 2¹¹⁄₁₆″ from the blade (measure to a tooth set toward the fence).

Both pieces come out 2¹¹⁄₁₆″ wide.

Safety
  • The board’s edge against the fence must be straight. Joint or rip a crooked edge straight first.
  • Use a push stick for the last 12″, and keep pushing until the wood is fully past the riving knife.
  • Support long boards at the outfeed end so they don’t tip up off the table.
Common mistake

Ripping a board with a curved (crowned) edge against the fence. The board rocks, the cut wanders, and it can bind. Put a straight edge on the fence, or snap a line and cut it straight with a circular saw first.

See also

Tilt the blade and rip along the fence to cut a bevel along a board’s length (a bevel rip), or just clip the corner (a chamfer). The rule for safety: the blade should tilt away from the fence. If it tilts toward the fence, the piece between the blade and the fence ends up underneath the tilted blade and boxed in by the fence, with nowhere to go but back at you.

Most saws tilt left (left-tilt), so the fence stays on the right. On a right-tilt saw, move the fence to the left side of the blade for bevel rips.

Where the blade meets the wood
width at the far face = width at the near face − T × tan(bevel)

Same offset formula as , with T = board thickness.

Worked example

Beveling both edges of ¾″ stock at 22.5° for an octagonal column wrap.

  1. Offset through ¾″: 0.75 × tan 22.5° = 0.31″ ≈ ⁵⁄₁₆″
  2. Each stave’s inside face is 2 × ⁵⁄₁₆″ = ⅝″ narrower than its outside face.
Safety
  • Blade tilts away from the fence. Re-check the riving knife clears at full tilt.
  • Tilted blades expose more tooth: keep push sticks in use for the whole cut.
Common mistake

Setting the fence distance with the blade vertical, then tilting. The tilt moves where the blade meets the table. Tilt first, then set the fence and make a test cut.

For crosscuts, the wood rides on the miter gauge, which slides in a miter slot. The fence stays out of the way. An auxiliary wooden face screwed to the gauge (and extending past the blade) supports the wood right up to the cut and holds a stop block.

Check the gauge’s scale. Some read 0° for a square cut, like a miter saw; others read 90° for square, like a protractor. The only way to know is to set it to its “square” mark and check against a square.

Worked example

You need a 30° saw setting (60° to the edge) and your gauge reads 90° at square.

  1. Its scale reads geometry: set it to 90 − 30 = 60°.
  2. On a gauge that reads 0° at square, you would set 30°.
Safety
  • Never use the rip fence as a length stop for a crosscut: the offcut gets trapped between blade and fence and is thrown. Clamp a stop block to the fence well in front of the blade instead, so the piece is free by the time it reaches the blade.
  • Hold the wood firmly to the gauge face; clamp it for angled cuts, where it tends to creep.
Common mistake

Trusting the gauge’s detents for picture frames. Gauges often have slop in the slot. For frames, a miter sled is much more accurate ().

A crosscut sled is a plywood base with runners in both miter slots and a fence square to the blade. The wood sits still on the sled while the whole sled slides, so it cuts wide boards square and safely.

The sled is only as good as its fence. The 5-cut method measures fence error very precisely: cut all four edges of a square panel in turn (rotating it the same way each time), then cut a thin strip off the first edge. Any error builds up four times, so the strip tapers by four times the error.

Formula
error per cut = (A − B) ÷ 4, over the strip length L
move the fence end = (A − B) ÷ 4 × fence length ÷ L

A and B = strip width at each end (use calipers). The fence pivots about one end.

Worked example

18″ square panel. The strip measures 0.265″ at one end and 0.253″ at the other. Fence is 24″ long.

  1. A − B = 0.012″
  2. Error per cut: 0.012 ÷ 4 = 0.003″ over 18″ (0.0095°)
  3. Move the fence end: 0.003 × 24 ÷ 18 = 0.004″

Four thousandths of an inch: a sheet of paper. Tap the fence, re-tighten, and repeat the test until the difference is under 0.004″.

Safety
  • Put a thick block on the back of the sled fence where the blade exits, so the blade never emerges from the fence.
  • Keep hands behind the fence line and out of the blade path; add a stop so the sled can’t be pushed far enough to expose the blade.
Common mistake

Rotating the panel the wrong way between cuts, or flipping it over. Keep the same face up and turn it the same direction every time, or the errors cancel instead of adding.

A miter sled has two fences at 45° to the blade and exactly 90° to each other, forming a V. Cut one end of a frame piece against the left fence and the mating end against the right fence. Because the fences are 90° apart, the two cuts always add up to 90°, even if each is slightly off 45°.

Worked example

The fences are 44.8° and 45.2° to the blade, but exactly 90° to each other.

  1. Mating ends: 44.8 + 45.2 = 90°
  2. Every corner closes square; only the joint line leans a tiny 0.2° from the diagonal.
Safety
  • Clamp short pieces to the fence; your hands stay outside the V.
Common mistake

Cutting both ends of every piece on the same fence. Then errors add instead of cancel. Always cut mating ends on opposite fences.

Tapers run at angles far too shallow to set on a scale, so a taper jig sets them by distance. The common kind is two boards hinged at one end, with an adjustable stop that holds them apart. The jig rides against the fence; the leg rides against the jig. Opening the jig by a measured gap at a known distance from the hinge sets the taper angle.

Formula
gap = distance from hinge × tan(taper angle)
or: gap at the leg’s length = material removed
Worked example

The leg from : ⅜″ off over 24″ (0.895°).

  1. Gap at 12″ from the hinge: 12 × tan 0.895° = 0.1875″ = ³⁄₁₆″
  2. Set the fence so the blade meets the leg exactly at the start mark.
  3. Cut the first inside face. Turn the leg 90° to the second inside face and cut again with the same setting.

For tapers on two opposite faces, the second cut needs double the gap, because the first tapered face now lies against the jig.

Safety
  • Clamp the leg to the jig (toggle clamps are ideal) so it can’t twist into the blade.
  • Push the jig, not the leg, and keep going until both are past the riving knife.
Common mistake

Measuring the gap at the far end of a jig that is longer than the leg and forgetting to scale. Measure the gap at the distance you calculated for, or recompute for where you measure.

Not every table-saw cut goes all the way through. Three flat-bottomed slots are common in cabinets and boxes:

  • Groove: a slot running with the grain (for a drawer bottom).
  • Dado: a slot across the grain (for a shelf end).
  • Rabbet: a step along an edge or end (for a cabinet back or a picture-frame recess).

Cut them with several passes of a regular blade, or with a dado stack: a set of blades and chippers that cut the full width in one pass (it needs its own throat plate).

Worked example

A ¼″-deep dado for a ¾″ shelf, cut with a ⅛″ blade.

  1. Blade height ¼″.
  2. Width ¾″: ¾ ÷ ⅛ = 6 overlapping passes, moving the stop ⅛″ each time (or one pass with a ¾″ dado stack).

A dado about ⅓ of the side’s thickness holds a shelf well without weakening the side.

Safety
  • For non-through cuts, follow your saw’s manual for the riving-knife position (many lower it to just below the blade top). A fixed splitter usually has to come off; put it back afterward.
  • Never cut a dado against the fence with a long board end-on; use the miter gauge or a sled.
Common mistake

Cutting the dado to the shelf’s nominal thickness. Plywood sold as ¾″ is often ²³⁄₃₂″. Measure the actual shelf and sneak up on the width with test cuts.

A 4×8 sheet of plywood weighs 50–70 pounds and is too big to control on most table saws alone. The reliable approach: break it down into manageable pieces with a circular saw first (), then make the final, accurate cuts on the table saw.

If you do rip full sheets on the table saw, you need support on three sides: an outfeed table behind the saw and a side support on the left, all level with the table.

Worked example

Cabinet sides 23¼″ deep from a 48″-wide sheet.

  1. Circular saw: rough-cut the sheet into two pieces about 24″ wide.
  2. Table saw: factory edge against the fence, rip each to 23¼″.
Safety
  • Keep the sheet flat and tight to the fence the whole cut; a sheet that lifts or twists can kick back with a lot of mass behind it.
  • Get help or supports; never catch a falling sheet with the blade running.
Common mistake

Using a cut edge as the reference for the next rip. Use the straight factory edge against the fence for the first rip of each piece.

Blades differ in tooth count, tooth shape and thickness:

  • Rip blades (about 24 teeth on 10″): few, flat-topped teeth (FTG) that chisel along the grain fast.
  • Crosscut blades (60–80 teeth): many alternating bevel teeth (ATB) that slice across fibres cleanly.
  • Combination (40–50 teeth): a compromise that stays on the saw most of the time.
  • Kerf: full-kerf blades cut about ⅛″; thin-kerf about ³⁄₃₂″, easier for low-power saws but they flex more.
Worked example

Building a set of cabinets from plywood and solid-wood face frames.

  1. Plywood and face-frame crosscuts: 60–80T ATB to avoid splintering the veneer.
  2. Ripping 8/4 hardwood: 24T rip blade, which won’t burn or bog down.
Safety
  • Unplug before changing blades. Use a blade rated for your saw’s RPM, and check the teeth point toward you at the top of the blade.
Common mistake

Ripping thick hardwood with a fine crosscut blade. The small gullets clog, the blade heats and burns the wood, and you push harder, which is when accidents happen.

The circular saw is the framer’s workhorse: you move the saw, not the wood, so it cuts anything you can reach, from 2×12s to full sheets. Its blade spins so the teeth come up through the wood from underneath, which means splinters appear on the top face. Cut with the good face down.

  • Depth: set the blade about ¼″ below the bottom of the board. Less blade exposed, less kickback, cleaner cut.
  • Bevel: the base plate (shoe) tilts from 0° to 45° or more; the scale reads 0° with the blade square to the base.
  • Sight line: a notch at the front of the base marks the blade line. Check it against a test cut; it’s often slightly off.
Worked example

Cutting a 2×10 (1½″ thick).

  1. Depth: 1½ + ¼ = 1¾″.
  2. Unplugged, hold the saw beside the board edge and set the depth so the teeth hang ¼″ below.
Safety
  • Two hands on the saw; the blade guard must snap back freely.
  • Stand to the side of the cut line, not behind the saw.
  • Let the blade stop before lifting the saw out of the kerf.
Common mistake

Leaving the blade at full depth for thin stock. Several inches of exposed teeth under the board catch clamps, cords and bench tops, and make kickback violent.

For square and angled crosscuts on framing lumber, hold a speed square’s lip against the far edge of the board and run the saw’s base plate along the square’s edge. It is fast, accurate enough for framing, and works at any angle the square can pivot to.

  1. Mark the line. Set the blade to the line on the waste side.
  2. Slide the square up against the base plate’s edge; hook its lip on the board edge; hold it firmly.
  3. Cut, keeping the base plate against the square the whole way.
Worked example

A 22.5° miter on a 2×6: pivot the square to 22.5 on its degree scale (), hold it, and guide the saw along it.

Safety
  • Keep the hand holding the square behind it, well away from the blade path.
Common mistake

Lining up the square with the pencil line instead of the saw. The square guides the base plate, which is offset from the blade. Set the blade on the line first, then bring the square to the plate.

How the board is supported decides whether a circular-saw cut goes smoothly or pinches. If a board spans two supports and you cut between them, it sags into a V and the kerf closes on the blade: the saw binds and can kick back toward you.

Support the wood so the kerf opens as you cut: cut near the end of an overhang (let a short offcut fall), or support both sides right next to the cut (a sheet of rigid foam under the whole sheet works well).

Worked example

Cutting 2 feet off an 8-foot 2×6 on sawhorses.

  1. Put the horses under the 6-foot keeper, one of them close to the cut.
  2. Cut so the 2-foot offcut overhangs and drops away.
Safety
  • If the saw starts to bind, release the trigger and hold the saw still until it stops. Don’t back it out while spinning.
Common mistake

Supporting both ends of a long offcut and cutting in the middle. Both halves sag toward the cut and pinch the blade.

For long straight cuts (ripping a sheet, trimming a door), clamp a straightedge to the work as a fence for the saw’s base plate. The straightedge sits away from the cut line by the base offset: the distance from the edge of the base plate to the blade.

Formula
straightedge position = cut line + base offset (on the keeper side)
Worked example

Ripping 23½″ off a sheet; the saw’s base offset is 1½″ on the left side.

  1. Snap the cut line at 23½″ ().
  2. Clamp the straightedge 1½″ from the line, on the keeper side.
  3. Cut with the base plate tight to the straightedge.
Safety
  • Support the sheet on foam or across several joists so both sides stay supported; kneel on the sheet only if the saw’s path is clear of you.
Common mistake

Measuring the base offset from the wrong side of the blade teeth. Make a test cut along a straightedge on scrap and measure from the straightedge to the kerf.

A Western hand saw cuts on the push stroke; its teeth lean away from the handle. A Japanese saw cuts on the pull stroke; its thinner blade stays straight because it is in tension, so it cuts a finer kerf. Both come in crosscut and rip tooth patterns (many Japanese ryoba saws have one of each on opposite edges).

Starting is the hard part. Guide the blade with your thumb knuckle, start with a few short strokes at a low angle on the waste side of the line, then lengthen the strokes.

Worked example

A 60° saw setting on a triangle frame is beyond most miter saws ().

  1. Mark the line with a protractor and square it across the edge.
  2. Start with the pull saw at the far corner, then lower it to follow both lines at once.
Safety
  • Clamp the work. Keep the guiding thumb above the teeth, and move it away once the kerf is started.
Common mistake

Forcing the saw. Let its weight do the cutting; pushing hard makes it wander and bind.

A miter box is a three-sided box (or a metal frame) with slots that guide a hand saw at fixed angles, usually 90°, 45° and sometimes 22.5°. It is quiet, cheap and accurate enough for small trim and picture frames.

Worked example

A small frame from 1×2 stock.

  1. Hold the piece against the back wall of the box.
  2. Cut with the saw in the 45° slot; use the opposite 45° slot for the mating end.
Common mistake

Letting the work sit away from the back wall. The angle is only right when the work is tight against the box’s back.

A jigsaw has a short blade that moves up and down. It cuts curves, holes and notches, and its base tilts for bevels. Most blades cut on the upstroke, so splinters come up on the top face: cut with the good face down, or use a reverse-tooth blade.

For tight curves, make relief cuts from the edge to the line first so waste falls away in small pieces instead of binding the blade.

Worked example

A 3″-radius round corner on a shelf.

  1. Trace the curve. Make three relief cuts from the edge to within ¹⁄₁₆″ of the line.
  2. Cut the curve; the waste falls away in pieces. Sand to the line.
Safety
  • Keep the base flat on the work and the blade clear of the bench, clamps and cords underneath.
Common mistake

Turning the saw too sharply. The blade twists, the cut goes out of square and the blade may snap. If it won’t follow, back out and add relief cuts.

A coping saw holds a very thin blade in a C-shaped frame. The blade can be turned in the frame, so it follows tight curves, and it is the tool for coping trim joints (). Most people mount the blade with the teeth pointing toward the handle so it cuts on the pull stroke.

Worked example

Coping a baseboard with a 15–20 teeth-per-inch blade: tension the blade until it rings when plucked, tilt the saw to back-cut about 30°, and follow the profile line.

Common mistake

A slack blade. It wanders and snaps. Tighten the handle until the blade is taut.
