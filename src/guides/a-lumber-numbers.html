Start here if you are new. This guide names the parts of a cut and a board, explains why a “2×4” isn’t 2″ by 4″, and gets you fluent with the sixteenths of an inch that every other guide uses.

Key points

  • Crosscuts shorten a board, rips narrow it. Miters angle across the face, bevels through the thickness.
  • Use actual sizes in every calculation: a 2×4 is 1½″ × 3½″.
  • Wood moves across the grain with humidity, hardly at all along it.
  • Decimal to sixteenths: multiply the part after the point by 16 and round.

Wood has grain: long fibres that run the length of the board, like a bundle of straws. Every cut is named by how it crosses those fibres.

  • A crosscut goes across the grain. It makes a board shorter. Every angled cut in this guide is a kind of crosscut.
  • A rip cut goes along the grain. It makes a board narrower.
Common mistake

Using a rip blade for crosscuts (or the reverse). Rip blades have few, large teeth and tear the fibres when crosscutting, which leaves a ragged miter. For clean angled cuts, use a crosscut or combination blade with more teeth.

A plain crosscut is square: straight across, at 90° to the edges. An angled crosscut leans in one of two directions, or both.

  • A miter is angled across the face. Looking down at the board, the end is slanted. Picture frames use miters.
  • A bevel is angled through the thickness. Looking down, the end looks square, but it slopes when you look at the edge.
  • A compound cut is both at once: slanted across the face and sloped through the thickness. Crown molding and splayed boxes need these.

On a miter saw, the miter setting swings the saw left and right; the bevel setting tilts the blade over.

Common mistake

Setting the bevel when you meant the miter. Both scales read 45° for a picture-frame corner, so the numbers look the same. Ask: “Should the slant show when I look down at the face (miter), or when I look at the edge (bevel)?”

See also

An angled cut makes one edge of the board longer than the other. The tip of the longer edge is the long point (the sharp corner). The tip of the shorter edge is the short point (the blunt corner, also called the heel).

Carpenters measure mitered parts “long point to long point” because the sharp tip is easy to hook a tape on and easy to see. On a frame, the long points sit on the outside edge and the short points on the inside edge.

Common mistake

Writing “cut to 20 inches” on a cut list without saying which edge. A 2×4 mitered at 45° is 7″ shorter on one edge than the other, so “20 inches” can mean two very different parts. Always write LP (long point) or SP (short point) next to the length.

A saw blade has thickness. The slot it leaves behind is the kerf, usually about ⅛″ for a full-size circular or miter saw blade (thin-kerf blades are around ³⁄₃₂″). That wood turns to sawdust, so it is gone from both pieces.

The rule that follows: put the kerf on the waste side of your line. The part you keep is called the keeper; the other part is the waste (or offcut).

Formula
material used = Σ(piece lengths) + (number of cuts) × kerf

Σ means “add them all up”.

Worked example

You want four 18″ pieces from an 8-foot (96″) 2×4, cut with a ⅛″ kerf.

  1. Pieces: 4 × 18″ = 72″
  2. Cuts: 4 (the last one frees the fourth piece from the leftover): 4 × ⅛″ = ½″
  3. Total used: 72½″

You use 72½″ and have 23½″ left over. Kerf only matters here when the board is tight: five 19″ pieces (95″ plus five ⅛″ kerfs = 95⅝″) would not fit in a 96″ board with room to square the end.

Common mistake

Marking all the pieces along the board at 18″, 36″, 54″, 72″ and then cutting. Each cut eats into the next piece, so pieces 2, 3 and 4 come out short. Measure and mark one piece at a time from the freshly cut end.

Lumber is named by its nominal size: the rough size of the board before it was dried and planed smooth. What you take home is the actual size, which is smaller. A “2×4” starts life around 2″ × 4″ and ends up 1½″ × 3½″.

For softwood construction lumber the pattern is regular: 1× boards are ¾″ thick, 2× boards are 1½″ thick. Widths lose ½″ up to 6″ nominal and ¾″ from 8″ up. Every formula in this handbook uses actual sizes.

Common softwood lumber sizes
Rule
thickness: 1× → ¾″ · 2× → 1½″ · 4× → 3½″
width: nominal − ½″ (up to 6″) · nominal − ¾″ (8″ and up)
Worked example

How thick is a 2×6 wall with ½″ drywall on both sides?

  1. 2×6 actual width: 5½″
  2. Drywall: 2 × ½″ = 1″

The finished wall is 6½″ thick, not the 8″ you might guess from the names.

Common mistake

Designing with nominal sizes. A bookshelf planned with “1×8” shelves that are 8″ deep comes out 7¼″ deep, and every joint that depends on that width is off by ¾″.

A board has three kinds of surface. The two wide ones are faces, the two narrow ones are edges, and the two cut ones are ends. The ends show end grain: the cut-off tips of the wood fibres, with the tree’s growth rings showing as curved lines.

How the board was sawn from the log decides which way those rings run. In flatsawn boards (the usual construction lumber) the rings run across the end in arcs; in quartersawn boards they run roughly straight through the thickness. Quartersawn wood stays flatter and moves less.

Why it matters
  • End grain soaks up glue and finish and splits easily near the end. Keep nails and screws back from ends.
  • A flatsawn board tends to cup away from the heart of the tree (the rings try to straighten as they dry).
  • Measure a miter on the face, a bevel on the edge ().
Worked example

You are gluing up a tabletop from flatsawn boards.

  1. Look at each end and note which way the rings arc.
  2. Alternate the arcs (up, down, up) so any cupping of neighbouring boards partly cancels.

The top stays flatter over the seasons than one where every board cups the same way.

Common mistake

Driving a nail 1″ from the end of a 2×4. End grain splits easily; keep fasteners at least 1″ to 1½″ from ends, or pre-drill.

Few boards are perfectly straight. The four ways a board departs from flat have names:

  • Crown: a curve along the length when you sight down the edge. Framers put the crown up on joists and rafters, so loads flatten it.
  • Bow: a curve along the length when you sight down the face.
  • Cup: a curve across the width, like a shallow trough.
  • Twist: the corners don’t lie in one plane, like a propeller.

To check a board, hold one end at eye level and sight along the edge, then along the face. Mark the crown with an arrow or a “C” so you install it consistently.

Worked example

Stretch a string from end to end along the edge of an 8-foot 2×8 joist and measure the biggest gap.

  1. Gap in the middle: ¼″ on the top side.
  2. That is the crown. Mark an arrow pointing to it.

Install it arrow up. Floor load pushes it down toward straight instead of making a dip.

Common mistake

Mixing crown-up and crown-down joists in a floor. The subfloor then rolls like a wave. Sight every joist and install every crown the same way.

Wood takes on and gives off moisture with the seasons. As it does, it swells and shrinks across the grain (width and thickness) but barely at all along it. A 5½″ board can change width by ¹⁄₁₆″ or more between summer and winter while its length stays put.

How much it moves depends on the species, how it was sawn, and how far the moisture content (MC: the weight of water as a percentage of the dry wood) changes. Indoor wood typically swings 3–5 points of MC over a year.

Formula
change in width ≈ W × C × ΔMC

W = width across the grain. C = movement coefficient per 1% moisture change (about 0.0025–0.0035 for many flatsawn species; roughly half that for quartersawn). ΔMC = change in moisture content, in percentage points.

Worked example

A 5½″ flatsawn board, C = 0.0030, moisture swings 4 points over the year.

  1. 5.5 × 0.0030 × 4 = 0.066″ ≈ ¹⁄₁₆″

A tabletop of five such boards (27½″ wide) moves about ⅓″. Fasten it with clips or slotted holes, never rigid glue blocks.

Common mistake

Gluing a wide solid-wood panel tightly into a frame. When it shrinks it cracks; when it swells it pushes the joints apart. This is also why wide miters open up ().

A tape measure splits each inch into 16 parts. The tick marks get shorter as the fraction gets finer, so you can read a mark by its length:

  • longest: the inch, with its number
  • next: ½″
  • next: ¼″ and ¾″
  • next: the eighths (⅛, ⅜, ⅝, ⅞)
  • shortest: the sixteenths (¹⁄₁₆, ³⁄₁₆ …)

Most tapes also print every 16″ in red (the usual stud spacing) and put a small black diamond every 19³⁄₁₆″ (truss spacing: five per 8 feet).

Method
reading = whole inch + (ticks past it) ⁄ 16

Count sixteenth-ticks past the last inch, then reduce the fraction: 4⁄16 = ¼, 10⁄16 = ⅝.

Worked example
  1. The mark is past the 2″ line.
  2. It is 5 sixteenth-ticks past it (one past the ¼″ mark).
  3. 2 + 5⁄16 can’t be reduced.

The reading is 2⁵⁄₁₆″.

Common mistake

Counting from the wrong side of the inch line, so a reading of 2⁵⁄₁₆ becomes 3⁵⁄₁₆ or 1¹¹⁄₁₆. Always find the last whole inch before the mark first.

Calculators give decimals (2.28″); tapes show fractions. To convert, you only need to know how many sixteenths the decimal part is. Multiply it by 16 and round to the nearest whole number.

For finish work you may want thirty-seconds (multiply by 32). For framing, the nearest sixteenth is plenty.

Formula
sixteenths = round(decimal part × 16)
decimal = sixteenths ÷ 16
Sixteenths of an inch as decimals
Worked example
  1. 2.28″: 0.28 × 16 = 4.48 → 4 sixteenths = ¼ → 2¼″
  2. 6.06″: 0.06 × 16 = 0.96 → 1 sixteenth → 6¹⁄₁₆″
  3. 16.04″: 0.04 × 16 = 0.64 → 1 → 16¹⁄₁₆″
Common mistake

Reading 2.28″ as “2 and 28 hundredths, so about 2¼ and a bit… 2⅜?” Decimals and sixteenths don’t line up by eye. Do the multiplication, or use the chart above.

Cut lists need fraction arithmetic: subtracting two offsets, adding a kerf, finding the middle of a board. The trick is to put everything in sixteenths first, do plain whole-number math, then convert back.

Halving is the most common job (finding a center mark). Half of an even number of sixteenths is easy; for an odd number, go to thirty-seconds.

Method
a ± b: convert both to sixteenths → add or subtract → convert back
half of n⁄16 = n⁄32
Worked example
  1. Center of 23⅝″: 23⅝ = 378⁄16; half = 189⁄16 = 11¹³⁄₁₆″
  2. 31⅜″ minus two 3½″ offsets: 502⁄16 − 112⁄16 = 390⁄16 = 24⅜″
  3. Half of 5⁄16″: 5⁄32″ (a hair over ⅛″)
Common mistake

Halving the whole number and the fraction separately: half of 23⅝ is not 11 plus half of ⅝ (that forgets the leftover ½″ from 23). Convert first.

Lumber is sold by the foot (8′, 10′, 12′…), plans mix feet and inches, and saws are set in inches. Convert everything to inches while you plan: 1′ = 12″.

A cut list is the table of every piece you need: name, quantity, length, and for angled parts the angle and whether the length is long point (LP) or short point (SP). Laying the pieces out on the boards you will buy, with kerfs and a trimmed factory end, tells you how many boards to buy.

Assuming the factory end is square. It often isn’t, and it may be split or dirty. Trim ½″ off before measuring your first piece, and include that in the layout.
