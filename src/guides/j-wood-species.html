Before you start

These pages use the hardness, strength, stiffness, shrinkage, rot and working ratings explained in . Read first; the rest of Guide I explains each number in more depth.

The species are grouped the way you will meet them: construction softwoods from Alberta and BC, the hardwoods sold by Calgary dealers, the light hardwoods carvers love, and three popular imports. To compare them side by side, see .

Strengths

  • Cheap, light and sold everywhere.
  • Straight and easy to cut, nail and screw.
  • Stiff enough for studs and joists, with a good strength-to-weight ratio.

Weaknesses

  • Soft: a fingernail dents it.
  • Knots, wane, bow and twist in the lower grades.
  • Rots quickly if it stays wet; stains blotchy.

For builders

The framing wood of Alberta: studs, plates, joists, rafters, blocking, jigs and shop furniture.

For carvers and turners

Poor for detail, because the latewood lines are harder than the wood between them. Fine for rough shapes, practice and painted work.

Shop tip

Sight down every board before you buy it. Crown, bow and twist are normal in SPF; build joists and rafters crown up.

Strengths

  • Alberta-grown, cheap and light.
  • Beetle-killed “blue pine” looks striking at no extra cost.
  • Straight and easy to work.

Weaknesses

  • Soft and easily dented.
  • Small knots and pitch pockets.
  • Rots if kept wet.

For builders

Framing (sold as SPF), log buildings, fence rails and poles, panelling. Blue-stained boards make feature walls and ceilings.

For carvers and turners

Carves reasonably when clear, but pitch gums up tools. Tears on the lathe.

Shop tip

The blue stain is a fungus carried by the mountain pine beetle. It colours the wood but doesn’t rot or weaken it, so the boards are fine to use indoors.

Strengths

  • The stiffest, strongest common softwood: as stiff as red oak at about 70% of the weight.
  • Holds nails and screws well.
  • Comes in long, clear, large timbers; vertical-grain stock stays straight.

Weaknesses

  • Splinters easily, and the splinters fester.
  • Hard latewood and soft earlywood: tears out and stains unevenly.
  • Splits when nailed near the ends.

For builders

Beams, posts, headers, timber frames, joists, stair stringers and treads. Vertical-grain fir makes handsome doors, windows and trim.

For carvers and turners

Hard to carve or turn cleanly because of the uneven rings. Wire-brushing raises the latewood into a textured surface that sculptors use.

Shop tip

Pre-drill near ends, and use a sharp crosscut blade with a zero-clearance insert to stop splintering on the exit side.

Strengths

  • Soft, light and very even: the classic hand-tool and carving softwood.
  • Stable for a softwood (8.2% volumetric shrinkage).
  • Glues and paints well; clear grades are knot-free.

Weaknesses

  • Dents easily.
  • Blotches under stain.
  • Knots bleed resin through paint unless sealed.

For builders

Interior trim, doors, windows, panelling, drawer boxes, painted furniture. Make shelves thicker: it isn’t stiff.

For carvers and turners

A favourite for signs, decoys, relief and figure carving; it cuts crisply with sharp tools. Too soft for crisp spindle details.

Shop tip

Seal knots with a shellac-based primer before painting, or the resin bleeds yellow through the paint.

Strengths

  • Rot- and insect-resistant heartwood.
  • The most stable wood in this handbook (6.8% volumetric shrinkage); lies flat.
  • Very light, with a pleasant smell.

Weaknesses

  • Very soft and weak; splits easily.
  • Tannins stain and corrode plain steel fasteners (black streaks).
  • Dust is a strong sensitizer.

For builders

Deck boards, fences, siding, shingles, pergolas, saunas, closets and garden beds. Not for structural framing where strength matters.

For carvers and turners

The traditional wood of Northwest Coast poles and masks. Carves easily with very sharp tools but the soft earlywood crushes; too soft for fine turning.

Shop tip

Use stainless or hot-dip galvanized screws, and don’t overdrive them: the heads sink into the soft wood.

Strengths

  • Straight and even; clear grades are knot-free.
  • Stiffer and stronger than spruce.
  • Low in resin; takes paint and stain evenly for a softwood.

Weaknesses

  • Not durable outdoors.
  • Can split when nailed.
  • Wet pockets can leave kiln-dried boards unevenly dry.

For builders

Framing (as hem-fir), timbers, interior trim, doors, windows, mouldings and clear flooring.

For carvers and turners

Carves reasonably, but white pine and cedar are better. Not a turning wood.

Shop tip

Pre-drill near board ends, and check moisture with a meter before finishing: wet pockets can shrink after installation.

Strengths

  • Very hard and wear-resistant.
  • Fine, closed pores: good for cutting boards and butcher block.
  • Stunning figure: curly, quilted, birdseye.

Weaknesses

  • Heavy; dulls tools and burns on saws and router bits.
  • Blotches under stain.
  • Moves a lot (14.7% volumetric shrinkage).

For builders

Flooring, workbench tops, cutting boards, countertops, stair treads, cabinet frames and tool handles.

For carvers and turners

Too hard to carve easily, but superb on the lathe: crisp beads and a glass-smooth finish. Classic for rolling pins and handles.

Shop tip

Keep the feed moving and blades clean. Maple scorches if it stops against a spinning blade or bit.

Strengths

  • Machines and turns well.
  • Cheaper than hard maple.
  • Fine texture that takes paint beautifully.

Weaknesses

  • About ⅔ the hardness of hard maple.
  • Grey mineral streaks.
  • Blotches under stain.

For builders

Painted cabinets and furniture, drawer sides, interior trim and secondary parts.

For carvers and turners

Carves more easily than hard maple and turns well. Spalted soft maple is a turner’s favourite.

Shop tip

For painted work that will take knocks, soft maple resists dents better than poplar for a little more money.

Strengths

  • Hard, strong and inexpensive.
  • Takes stain evenly.
  • Sold everywhere; steam-bends well.

Weaknesses

  • Open pores wick water: it rots quickly outdoors.
  • Coarse; pores show through paint without grain filler.
  • Turns black where steel and water meet its tannins.

For builders

Floors, stair treads and handrails, trim, cabinets, furniture and interior doors.

For carvers and turners

Coarse and splintery for carving; good for bold shapes, poor for fine detail. Turns acceptably.

Shop tip

Red or white? Blow through the end grain of a short piece. Red oak’s open pores let air (and soap bubbles) through; white oak’s are plugged.
