Start here if you are new. This guide names the parts of a cut and a board, explains why a “2×4” isn’t 2″ by 4″, and gets you fluent with the sixteenths of an inch that every other guide uses.

Key points

  • Crosscuts shorten a board, rips narrow it. Miters angle across the face, bevels through the thickness.
  • Use actual sizes in every calculation: a 2×4 is 1½″ × 3½″.
  • Wood moves across the grain with humidity, hardly at all along it.
  • Decimal to sixteenths: multiply the part after the point by 16 and round.

Wood has grain: long fibres that run the length of the board, like a bundle of straws. Every cut is named by how it crosses those fibres.

  • A crosscut goes across the grain. It makes a board shorter. Every angled cut in this guide is a kind of crosscut.
  • A rip cut goes along the grain. It makes a board narrower.
Common mistake

Using a rip blade for crosscuts (or the reverse). Rip blades have few, large teeth and tear the fibres when crosscutting, which leaves a ragged miter. For clean angled cuts, use a crosscut or combination blade with more teeth.

See also

A plain crosscut is square: straight across, at 90° to the edges. An angled crosscut leans in one of two directions, or both.

  • A miter is angled across the face. Looking down at the board, the end is slanted. Picture frames use miters.
  • A bevel is angled through the thickness. Looking down, the end looks square, but it slopes when you look at the edge.
  • A compound cut is both at once: slanted across the face and sloped through the thickness. Crown molding and splayed boxes need these.

On a miter saw, the miter setting swings the saw left and right; the bevel setting tilts the blade over.

Common mistake

Setting the bevel when you meant the miter. Both scales read 45° for a picture-frame corner, so the numbers look the same. Ask: “Should the slant show when I look down at the face (miter), or when I look at the edge (bevel)?”

See also

An angled cut makes one edge of the board longer than the other. The tip of the longer edge is the long point (the sharp corner). The tip of the shorter edge is the short point (the blunt corner, also called the heel).

Carpenters measure mitered parts “long point to long point” because the sharp tip is easy to hook a tape on and easy to see. On a frame, the long points sit on the outside edge and the short points on the inside edge.

Common mistake

Writing “cut to 20 inches” on a cut list without saying which edge. A 2×4 mitered at 45° is 7″ shorter on one edge than the other, so “20 inches” can mean two very different parts. Always write LP (long point) or SP (short point) next to the length.

A saw blade has thickness. The slot it leaves behind is the kerf, usually about ⅛″ for a full-size circular or miter saw blade (thin-kerf blades are around ³⁄₃₂″). That wood turns to sawdust, so it is gone from both pieces.

The rule that follows: put the kerf on the waste side of your line. The part you keep is called the keeper; the other part is the waste (or offcut).

Formula
material used = Σ(piece lengths) + (number of cuts) × kerf

Σ means “add them all up”.

Worked example

You want four 18″ pieces from an 8-foot (96″) 2×4, cut with a ⅛″ kerf.

  1. Pieces: 4 × 18″ = 72″
  2. Cuts: 4 (the last one frees the fourth piece from the leftover): 4 × ⅛″ = ½″
  3. Total used: 72½″

You use 72½″ and have 23½″ left over. Kerf only matters here when the board is tight: five 19″ pieces (95″ plus five ⅛″ kerfs = 95⅝″) would not fit in a 96″ board with room to square the end.

Common mistake

Marking all the pieces along the board at 18″, 36″, 54″, 72″ and then cutting. Each cut eats into the next piece, so pieces 2, 3 and 4 come out short. Measure and mark one piece at a time from the freshly cut end.

Lumber is named by its nominal size: the rough size of the board before it was dried and planed smooth. What you take home is the actual size, which is smaller. A “2×4” starts life around 2″ × 4″ and ends up 1½″ × 3½″.

For softwood construction lumber the pattern is regular: 1× boards are ¾″ thick, 2× boards are 1½″ thick. Widths lose ½″ up to 6″ nominal and ¾″ from 8″ up. Every formula in this handbook uses actual sizes.

Common softwood lumber sizes
Rule
thickness: 1× → ¾″ · 2× → 1½″ · 4× → 3½″
width: nominal − ½″ (up to 6″) · nominal − ¾″ (8″ and up)
Worked example

How thick is a 2×6 wall with ½″ drywall on both sides?

  1. 2×6 actual width: 5½″
  2. Drywall: 2 × ½″ = 1″

The finished wall is 6½″ thick, not the 8″ you might guess from the names.

Common mistake

Designing with nominal sizes. A bookshelf planned with “1×8” shelves that are 8″ deep comes out 7¼″ deep, and every joint that depends on that width is off by ¾″.

A board has three kinds of surface. The two wide ones are faces, the two narrow ones are edges, and the two cut ones are ends. The ends show end grain: the cut-off tips of the wood fibres, with the tree’s growth rings showing as curved lines.

How the board was sawn from the log decides which way those rings run. In flatsawn boards (the usual construction lumber) the rings run across the end in arcs; in quartersawn boards they run roughly straight through the thickness. Quartersawn wood stays flatter and moves less.

Why it matters
  • End grain soaks up glue and finish and splits easily near the end. Keep nails and screws back from ends.
  • A flatsawn board tends to cup away from the heart of the tree (the rings try to straighten as they dry).
  • Measure a miter on the face, a bevel on the edge ().
Worked example

You are gluing up a tabletop from flatsawn boards.

  1. Look at each end and note which way the rings arc.
  2. Alternate the arcs (up, down, up) so any cupping of neighbouring boards partly cancels.

The top stays flatter over the seasons than one where every board cups the same way.

Common mistake

Driving a nail 1″ from the end of a 2×4. End grain splits easily; keep fasteners at least 1″ to 1½″ from ends, or pre-drill.

See also

Few boards are perfectly straight. The four ways a board departs from flat have names:

  • Crown: a curve along the length when you sight down the edge. Framers put the crown up on joists and rafters, so loads flatten it.
  • Bow: a curve along the length when you sight down the face.
  • Cup: a curve across the width, like a shallow trough.
  • Twist: the corners don’t lie in one plane, like a propeller.

To check a board, hold one end at eye level and sight along the edge, then along the face. Mark the crown with an arrow or a “C” so you install it consistently.

Worked example

Stretch a string from end to end along the edge of an 8-foot 2×8 joist and measure the biggest gap.

  1. Gap in the middle: ¼″ on the top side.
  2. That is the crown. Mark an arrow pointing to it.

Install it arrow up. Floor load pushes it down toward straight instead of making a dip.

Common mistake

Mixing crown-up and crown-down joists in a floor. The subfloor then rolls like a wave. Sight every joist and install every crown the same way.

See also

Wood takes on and gives off moisture with the seasons. As it does, it swells and shrinks across the grain (width and thickness) but barely at all along it. A 5½″ board can change width by ¹⁄₁₆″ or more between summer and winter while its length stays put.

How much it moves depends on the species, how it was sawn, and how far the moisture content (MC: the weight of water as a percentage of the dry wood) changes. Indoor wood typically swings 3–5 points of MC over a year.

Formula
change in width ≈ W × C × ΔMC

W = width across the grain. C = movement coefficient per 1% moisture change (about 0.0025–0.0035 for many flatsawn species; roughly half that for quartersawn). ΔMC = change in moisture content, in percentage points.

Worked example

A 5½″ flatsawn board, C = 0.0030, moisture swings 4 points over the year.

  1. 5.5 × 0.0030 × 4 = 0.066″ ≈ ¹⁄₁₆″

A tabletop of five such boards (27½″ wide) moves about ⅓″. Fasten it with clips or slotted holes, never rigid glue blocks.

Common mistake

Gluing a wide solid-wood panel tightly into a frame. When it shrinks it cracks; when it swells it pushes the joints apart. This is also why wide miters open up ().

A tape measure splits each inch into 16 parts. The tick marks get shorter as the fraction gets finer, so you can read a mark by its length:

  • longest: the inch, with its number
  • next: ½″
  • next: ¼″ and ¾″
  • next: the eighths (⅛, ⅜, ⅝, ⅞)
  • shortest: the sixteenths (¹⁄₁₆, ³⁄₁₆ …)

Most tapes also print every 16″ in red (the usual stud spacing) and put a small black diamond every 19³⁄₁₆″ (truss spacing: five per 8 feet).

Method
reading = whole inch + (ticks past it) ⁄ 16

Count sixteenth-ticks past the last inch, then reduce the fraction: 4⁄16 = ¼, 10⁄16 = ⅝.

Worked example
  1. The mark is past the 2″ line.
  2. It is 5 sixteenth-ticks past it (one past the ¼″ mark).
  3. 2 + 5⁄16 can’t be reduced.

The reading is 2⁵⁄₁₆″.

Common mistake

Counting from the wrong side of the inch line, so a reading of 2⁵⁄₁₆ becomes 3⁵⁄₁₆ or 1¹¹⁄₁₆. Always find the last whole inch before the mark first.

Calculators give decimals (2.28″); tapes show fractions. To convert, you only need to know how many sixteenths the decimal part is. Multiply it by 16 and round to the nearest whole number.

For finish work you may want thirty-seconds (multiply by 32). For framing, the nearest sixteenth is plenty.

Formula
sixteenths = round(decimal part × 16)
decimal = sixteenths ÷ 16
Sixteenths of an inch as decimals
Worked example
  1. 2.28″: 0.28 × 16 = 4.48 → 4 sixteenths = ¼ → 2¼″
  2. 6.06″: 0.06 × 16 = 0.96 → 1 sixteenth → 6¹⁄₁₆″
  3. 16.04″: 0.04 × 16 = 0.64 → 1 → 16¹⁄₁₆″
Common mistake

Reading 2.28″ as “2 and 28 hundredths, so about 2¼ and a bit… 2⅜?” Decimals and sixteenths don’t line up by eye. Do the multiplication, or use the chart above.

Cut lists need fraction arithmetic: subtracting two offsets, adding a kerf, finding the middle of a board. The trick is to put everything in sixteenths first, do plain whole-number math, then convert back.

Halving is the most common job (finding a center mark). Half of an even number of sixteenths is easy; for an odd number, go to thirty-seconds.

Method
a ± b: convert both to sixteenths → add or subtract → convert back
half of n⁄16 = n⁄32
Worked example
  1. Center of 23⅝″: 23⅝ = 378⁄16; half = 189⁄16 = 11¹³⁄₁₆″
  2. 31⅜″ minus two 3½″ offsets: 502⁄16 − 112⁄16 = 390⁄16 = 24⅜″
  3. Half of 5⁄16″: 5⁄32″ (a hair over ⅛″)
Common mistake

Halving the whole number and the fraction separately: half of 23⅝ is not 11 plus half of ⅝ (that forgets the leftover ½″ from 23). Convert first.

Lumber is sold by the foot (8′, 10′, 12′…), plans mix feet and inches, and saws are set in inches. Convert everything to inches while you plan: 1′ = 12″.

Every cut starts with a measurement and a mark. The first sections cover how to hold, read and trust each layout tool. The last two cover measuring the thing you actually need to cut (a corner, a slope, a length to the long point) and checking that what you built is square.

Key points

  • A tape’s hook slides on purpose: it corrects for its own thickness on inside and outside measurements.
  • Speed squares and saws read from square (0° = square line); protractors read from the edge (90° = square line).
  • Measure corners on the side the trim wraps around, then use saw = 90° − corner ÷ 2.
  • Any rectangle is square when its two diagonals are equal. 3-4-5 makes a right angle anywhere.
  • Check every tool once: flip a square, flip a level. Half the difference is the error.

The metal hook on the end of a tape measure is loose on purpose. It slides by exactly its own thickness. When you hook it over the end of a board and pull, it slides out; when you push it against a wall for an inside measurement, it slides in. Either way, the measurement starts from the true end. This is called true zero.

If the hook is bent or its rivets are worn, every reading is off by the same amount. For the most accurate work, burn an inch: measure from the 1″ mark instead of the hook, then subtract 1″ from the reading.

Method
burning an inch: length = reading − 1″
Worked example

You hold the 1″ mark on the end of a shelf and read 31⅜″ at the far end.

  1. 31⅜ − 1 = 30⅜″

The shelf is 30⅜″ long, measured without trusting the hook.

Common mistake

“Fixing” the loose hook by bending or tightening it. That makes every measurement wrong by the hook’s thickness. If two tapes disagree, compare them both against a steel rule, and use one tape for the whole project.

See also

A folding rule is rigid, so it measures inside spaces (between walls, inside a cabinet) without the sag or hook of a tape. Many have a brass extension slide for exact inside readings.

A story stick skips numbers altogether. You hold a straight stick against the real thing and mark the positions on the stick itself: shelf heights, the rise of each stair, where studs fall. Then you hold the stick against the new part and transfer the marks. No reading means no misreading.

  1. Cut a straight, clean stick a little longer than the part.
  2. Hold it tight against the reference and mark each position with a sharp pencil and a label (“S1”, “S2”).
  3. Hold it against the new part with the same end registered, and transfer each mark.
Worked example

Four shelves in a bookcase, with two sides that must match.

  1. Mark all four shelf heights on the stick from the first side.
  2. Transfer to the second side, with the stick’s bottom on the floor line both times.

Every shelf is level across the case, even if the plan’s numbers were rounded.

Common mistake

Registering the stick from different ends on the two parts. Mark “BOTTOM” on the stick and always start from the same reference edge.

The speed square (also called a rafter square) is a thick right triangle, usually 7″ or 12″, with a raised lip along one side. It is the framer’s everyday marking tool and, held against a board, a circular-saw guide.

  • Hook the lip on the board’s edge and the leg that runs across the board marks a square (90°) line.
  • The long side (hypotenuse) marks a 45° line.
  • The corner where the two short legs meet is the pivot. The degree scale runs along the hypotenuse; other scales (“COMMON”, “HIP-VAL”) give roof pitches.
  • Notches along the leg let you hold a pencil at a fixed distance from the edge for marking a line along the board.
Worked example

Marking a square crosscut on a 2×6 at 30″.

  1. Mark 30″ on the edge with a crow’s foot.
  2. Hook the lip on that edge, slide the square until its leg touches the mark.
  3. Draw along the leg across the face.
Common mistake

Letting the lip ride up on the edge (or on a splinter). The line then runs off square. Press the lip down and in with your fingers and pull the pencil toward you.

See also

For angles other than 90° and 45°, hold the pivot corner on the board’s edge at your mark and swing the square until the board’s edge crosses the degree scale at the angle you want. Then draw along the leg that runs from the pivot.

On most speed squares the degree scale reads like a saw: 0° gives a square line, and the number is how far the line leans away from square. Check yours once: pivot to 0 and compare the line with one drawn using the lip. If they match, your scale reads from square.

Worked example

Marking the end of a brace that needs a 40° saw setting ().

  1. Pivot on the edge at your mark.
  2. Swing until the edge reads 40 on the degree scale.
  3. Draw along the pivot leg. Check: the line should look like a cut the saw at 40° would make.
Common mistake

Drawing along the wrong edge of the square after pivoting. Only the leg that runs from the pivot corner gives the angle on the scale. The hypotenuse gives something else.

See also

The framing square (steel square) is a large L: a 24″ × 2″ blade and a 16″ × 1½″ tongue, meeting at the heel. It checks and marks square on big work (wall corners, sheet goods) and, with small clamp-on stair gauges, lays out repeated angles like stair steps and rafter cuts.

Its angle work is done by numbers, not degrees: set one number on the blade and another on the tongue against the edge of a board, and the square makes the angle of that rise and run.

Rule
blade number : tongue number = run : rise

Line up the blade at the run and the tongue at the rise on the same edge; the tongue then marks a plumb line and the blade a level line for that slope.

Worked example

Check a plywood sheet corner: hold the heel in the corner, blade along one edge, tongue along the other.

  1. Both edges touch the square along their full length: the corner is square.
  2. A gap opening toward the tongue’s end shows how far it is out over 16″.
Common mistake

Dropping the square. A framing square that has been dropped on its corner is often no longer square. Check it with the flip test () before trusting it.

See also

The combination square is a steel rule that slides through a cast head and locks anywhere along it. The head has a 90° face and a 45° face. It is more accurate than a speed square and does four jobs: square lines, 45° lines, depth measurement (lock the rule sticking out of the head), and marking a line parallel to an edge (hold a pencil at the end of the rule and slide the head along).

Any square can be checked with the flip test: draw a line along the blade, flip the square over and draw again from the same point. If the two lines don’t coincide, the square is off by half the gap.

Flip test
square’s error = gap between the two lines ÷ 2
Worked example

On the flip test, the two lines are ¹⁄₃₂″ apart at 6″ from the edge.

  1. Error: ¹⁄₃₂ ÷ 2 = ¹⁄₆₄″ over 6″
  2. As an angle: arctan(0.0156 ÷ 6) ≈ 0.15°

Fine for framing, not for furniture. Many squares can be trued by filing or tapping the head; otherwise, replace it.

Common mistake

Checking a square against another square. If both are off the same way, they agree and fool you. The flip test needs only a straight edge and a pencil.

See also

A protractor is the half-circle marked 0° to 180°. Put its center mark on the corner of the angle, line its base up with one side, and read where the other side crosses the scale.

Most protractors have two scales running in opposite directions. Use the scale that reads 0 on the side your baseline runs. A protractor reads geometry: a square line reads 90°, so convert before setting a saw ().

Conversion
saw setting = 90° − protractor reading

For an angle measured from the board’s edge to the cut line.

Worked example

A drawing shows a brace end at 62° from the edge.

  1. Protractor on the drawing: 62° (from the edge side).
  2. Saw: 90 − 62 = 28°
Common mistake

Reading the wrong scale and getting 118° instead of 62°. Ask whether the angle you are measuring is sharper or blunter than a square corner, and pick the number that matches.

See also

A protractor angle finder is two straight arms hinged at a round scale. Open the arms into a corner, lock them, and read the angle between them. It measures real corners in place, where a flat protractor can’t reach.

Many trim models print a second scale that shows the miter-saw setting directly (half the corner, converted to the saw scale). Read the label next to the scale so you know which number you are looking at.

Worked example
  1. Arms against both walls: the scale reads 135°.
  2. Saw: 90 − 135 ÷ 2 = 22.5°
  3. The miter scale on the tool (if it has one) should read 22.5.
Common mistake

Pressing the arms into the drywall tape and mud bulge right at the corner. Hold them a few inches out along each wall, where the wall is flat.

See also

The sliding T-bevel (bevel gauge) is a handle and a thin blade joined by a wing nut. It has no scale. It copies an angle from one place and carries it to another: from a corner to the wood, from a drawing to the saw, or from one leg to its three siblings.

Because nothing is read, nothing is misread. When you need a number, lay the locked bevel on a protractor.

  1. Loosen the wing nut, set the handle against one surface and the blade against the other, lock.
  2. On the wood: handle against the edge, draw along the blade.
  3. On a miter saw (unplugged): handle against the fence, swing the saw until the blade lies flat along the bevel’s blade.
Worked example

Four chair legs need the same splay angle as a sample leg.

  1. Set the bevel from the sample leg and lock it.
  2. Mark or check all four legs from the same bevel setting.

All four match the sample, and you never needed to know the number.

Common mistake

Knocking the blade while carrying the bevel. Tighten the wing nut firmly, and re-check the setting on the original if the bevel has been set down with other tools.

See also

A digital angle finder looks like the two-arm finder but shows the angle on a screen, often to 0.1°. Many have a hold button to freeze the reading when you can’t see the screen.

A digital angle gauge is a small magnetic cube that measures tilt relative to whatever you zeroed it on. Set it on the saw table, press zero, then stick it on the blade: it reads the blade’s tilt directly. It is the easiest way to set an exact bevel.

  1. Angle gauge: raise the blade fully, set the gauge on the table, press zero.
  2. Stick it on the blade body (not the teeth) and tilt the blade until the reading matches.
  3. Lock the tilt, then re-check: locking can shift it a few tenths.
Worked example

Setting a 43.1° bevel for the splayed planter in .

  1. Zero on the table. Stick to the blade. Tilt until it reads 43.1.
  2. Lock and re-read: 43.2. Nudge back and lock again.

A saw’s bevel scale can’t be read to a tenth; the gauge can.

Common mistake

Zeroing on the floor or a bench instead of the saw table. The gauge measures relative to wherever it was zeroed, so zero it on the surface the cut is measured from.

A level tells you whether a surface is horizontal (level) or vertical (plumb). The bubble sits between the lines when it is. The longer the level, the more it averages out small bumps, so use a 4′ level for walls and a 2′ level for shelves.

Check a level the same way as a square: read it, turn it end for end on the same spot, and read again. If the bubble moves, the level is off, and the true level is halfway between the two readings.

Rule of thumb
out of level over L = L × (rise per 12) ÷ 12

Lift the low end until the bubble centers; the gap under it is how far out the surface is over the level’s length.

Worked example

On a shelf, lifting one end of a 24″ level by ⅛″ centers the bubble.

  1. The shelf is ⅛″ out over 24″, so ¼″ over a 48″ shelf.
Common mistake

Checking a long wall with a short level held in one spot. Slide it along, or use a long level on a straight board, so a local bump doesn’t hide a lean.

A plumb bob is a pointed weight on a string. Gravity makes the string perfectly vertical, so it transfers a point straight down (from a ceiling mark to the floor) or checks a post for plumb over its whole height.

A laser level projects a level or plumb line across a room. It does the same job faster over long distances, but check it with a flip test occasionally, just like a spirit level.

Worked example

Placing a bottom plate directly under a top plate that is already nailed to the ceiling joists.

  1. Hang the bob from the plate’s edge at each end. Let it settle.
  2. Mark the floor under the point; snap a chalk line between the marks.

The studs will stand plumb between the plates.

Common mistake

Marking while the bob still swings, or in a draft. Let it settle, or steady it in a bucket of water.

See also

A chalk line is a string wound through powdered chalk. Stretch it tight between two marks and snap it: it leaves a perfectly straight line, as long as you need. Framers use it for plate lines on floors, cut lines on sheet goods and rows of roof shingles.

  1. Hook or hold the line on the first mark; pull it tight over the second.
  2. Lift the string straight up (not sideways) near the middle, a couple of inches.
  3. Let go. For long lines, have someone hold the middle down and snap each half.
Worked example

Ripping a 4×8 sheet to 23½″ wide with a circular saw.

  1. Mark 23½″ from the factory edge at both ends of the sheet.
  2. Snap a line between the marks, then cut on the waste side ().
Common mistake

Snapping sideways or with a slack line, which bows the line. Pull tight and lift straight up.

A contour gauge is a row of thin sliding pins in a holder. Press it against a shape (a molding profile, a door casing, a pipe) and the pins take the shape; lock them and trace the outline onto your board.

Worked example

Fitting a floor board around a door casing.

  1. Press the gauge against the casing at floor level; lock it.
  2. Trace the outline on the board, aligned with where the board will sit. Cut with a jigsaw or coping saw.
Common mistake

Pressing at an angle so the pins slide unevenly. Hold the gauge square to the surface and push straight in.

See also

Scribing copies the bumps of a wall onto a board so it can be cut to fit tight. Put the board in place (a countertop end, a shelf, a cabinet side), set a compass to the widest gap, then run the compass point along the wall with the pencil on the board. The pencil draws the wall’s shape, offset by the gap.

  1. Hold the board level and square in position, touching the wall at its closest point.
  2. Set the compass to the widest gap.
  3. Keep the compass legs horizontal (don’t tilt it) and trace along the wall.
  4. Cut to the line, back-beveling slightly so only the front edge touches.
Worked example

A shelf touches the wall at one point with a ¼″ gap at its widest.

  1. Set the compass to ¼″.
  2. Trace. Where the gap was widest, the line lands right on the board’s end: nothing comes off there. Where the board touched the wall, the line runs ¼″ in.
  3. Cut to the line and slide the shelf ¼″ toward the wall. It now touches all along.

The shelf ends up ¼″ further into the wall than where it first touched, so start with a board ¼″ long if the final length matters.

Common mistake

Tilting the compass as you go, which changes the distance. Keep both legs in a horizontal line the whole way.

Trim wraps around corners two ways. An inside corner points away from you (where two walls meet inside a room). An outside corner points toward you (the corner of a chimney breast or a hallway opening).

Measure the angle on the side the trim wraps around: inside corners from the room side, outside corners with the tool’s arms wrapped around the corner. Both give an angle under 180°, and both use the same saw formula.

Formula
saw = 90° − measured angle ÷ 2
Worked example
  1. Inside corner reads 91°: 90 − 45.5 = 44.5°
  2. Outside corner reads 88°: 90 − 44 = 46°

Neither is 45°. Walls that are 1–2° out are normal; see for which way the long point goes.

Measuring an outside corner from the room side and getting 270°, then halving that. Wrap the arms around the corner so they read the wall’s own angle.

To measure a slope (a sloped ceiling, a roof, a ramp, the line of existing stairs) you want either its pitch (rise per 12″) or its angle. Two quick ways:

  • Level and tape: hold a level horizontal with one end on the slope, and measure straight down from the level’s 12″ mark to the slope. That drop is the rise per 12.
  • Digital angle finder: lay it on the slope and read degrees directly.

The drop at 12″ is 3½″.

  1. Pitch: 3½-in-12.
  2. Angle: arctan(3.5 ÷ 12) = 16.26°

Measuring over 6″ and forgetting to double it. If you measure over any length other than 12″, scale it: rise per 12 = drop × 12 ÷ length.

A four-sided shape with equal opposite sides is a rectangle (square corners) exactly when its two diagonals are equal. This is the fastest way to check a frame, a cabinet, a wall or a deck, because you only need a tape.

If one diagonal is longer, push the two corners at the ends of the long diagonal toward each other until both diagonals match.

Formula
diagonal of a true rectangle = √(width² + height²)
fix: push the long diagonal’s corners by half the difference
Worked example

A 48″ × 30″ frame. Diagonals measure 56¾″ and 56½″.

  1. Target: √(48² + 30²) = 56.60″ ≈ 56⅝″
  2. Difference: ¼″. Push the corners of the 56¾″ diagonal together by about ⅛″.

Both diagonals now read 56⅝″ and every corner is square.

Common mistake

Checking diagonals before the opposite sides are equal. Diagonals only prove square on a shape whose opposite sides already match.

This is the math of angled cuts, always with the intuition first and a 2×4 as the running example. Every formula here is also on the page and in the .

Key points

  • Two pieces meeting at a corner each take half of it: saw = 90° − corner ÷ 2.
  • Polygon frames: saw = 180° ÷ number of sides.
  • Saw scales read from square (0° = square cut); geometry reads from the edge (90° = square cut).
  • Long point moves by W × tan(saw angle). At 45° it moves one full width.
  • Compound cuts: miter = atan(sin S · tan A), bevel = asin(cos S · sin A).

When two boards meet at a corner and both are cut, the joint line runs exactly through the middle of the corner. The fancy word is bisector: the line that splits an angle into two equal halves. Because the joint is shared, each board takes half of the corner.

Intuition: fold a sheet of paper so one edge lies on the other. The crease is the bisector, and it is where the two boards touch.

Formula
angle between cut and edge = C ÷ 2
saw setting = 90° − C ÷ 2

C is the corner angle: the angle between the two boards, measured on the side where they touch. The second line converts to a miter-saw reading, explained in .

Worked example

A square picture frame has four 90° corners.

  1. Angle between cut and edge: 90° ÷ 2 = 45°
  2. Saw setting: 90° − 45° = 45°

Every end gets a 45° miter. At 90° the two numbers happen to agree, which is why the saw’s different scale goes unnoticed until you try another corner.

Common mistake

Cutting one piece at the full corner angle and leaving the other square. That only works for a butt joint where one board lands on the face of another (see ). For a corner where both ends show, split the angle.

A regular polygon (a shape with n equal sides) has n equal corners. Walking around its outline you turn a full 360° in total, so each corner turns 360° ÷ n. Each corner is shared by two cuts, so each cut takes half of that turn: 180° ÷ n. That is the saw setting directly.

Formula
saw setting = 180° ÷ n
corner angle C = 180° − 360° ÷ n

n = number of sides. Check: 90° − C ÷ 2 gives the same saw setting.

Miter settings for polygon frames
Worked example

An octagonal mirror frame.

  1. n = 8
  2. Saw setting: 180° ÷ 8 = 22.5° (most miter saws have a click-stop, called a detent, right at 22.5°).
  3. Corner angle: 180° − 360° ÷ 8 = 135°. Each cut makes 67.5° with the edge.

Cut 16 ends (both ends of 8 pieces) at 22.5°, flipping the board between cuts so the long points land on the same edge.

Common mistake

Triangle frames need a 60° setting, and many miter saws stop at 45°–50°. Check your saw’s maximum before you buy wood. Beyond it, mark the line with a protractor and cut it with a handsaw, or use a jig that holds the board at an angle to the fence.

Real walls are rarely perfect. A bay window may have 135° corners, a hexagonal room 120°, and an ordinary “square” corner can easily be 91° or 92°. The half-angle rule still works: measure the real corner, halve it, convert to a saw setting.

Measure on the side where the boards will sit. For baseboard in a room, that is the room side of the walls. A sliding bevel or a digital angle finder does this well (see ).

Formula
saw setting = 90° − (measured corner ÷ 2)
Worked example
  1. Bay corner measures 135°: 90° − 135° ÷ 2 = 90° − 67.5° = 22.5°
  2. Hexagonal room corner 120°: 90° − 60° = 30°
  3. A “square” corner that measures 92°: 90° − 46° = 44°

The out-of-square wall needs 44°, not 45°. Cutting at 45° would leave a gap you can see.

Common mistake

Assuming a room corner is 90° because it looks square. A 2° error splits into 1° per piece, which opens about ¹⁄₁₆″ on a 3½″ board. Measure every corner; it takes seconds.

Not every frame is a regular polygon. A window seat in a bay, a countertop edge along crooked walls, or a frame around an odd-shaped mirror may have every corner at a different angle. The half-angle rule still works; you just apply it one joint at a time.

Check your measurements with one fact: the inside corners of any closed four-sided shape add up to 360° (five sides: 540°; in general (n − 2) × 180°). If yours don’t, one reading is off.

Formula
saw at each joint = 90° − (that corner ÷ 2)
check: sum of corners = (n − 2) × 180°
Worked example

Corners measured at 80°, 100°, 95° and 85°.

  1. Check: 80 + 100 + 95 + 85 = 360° ✓
  2. Joint 1: 90 − 40 = 50°; joint 2: 90 − 50 = 40°
  3. Joint 3: 90 − 47.5 = 42.5°; joint 4: 90 − 42.5 = 47.5°

The piece between joints 1 and 2 is cut 50° at one end and 40° at the other. Label each end on the wood (J1, J2…) before cutting, and use W × tan θ₁ + W × tan θ₂ for its length ().

Measuring one corner and assuming its opposite matches. In irregular shapes they rarely do. Measure and label every corner, and write the saw setting on both pieces that meet there.

When a board ends against the face of another board (a T-joint, or a brace landing on a post), only the arriving board gets cut. Its end must lie flat against the surface it meets, so the cut line is parallel to that surface. There is nothing to share, so that board takes the whole angle, not half.

A diagonal brace between a post and a beam is the classic case. It has two ends, one against each member, and its two saw settings always add up to 90° when the post and beam are square to each other.

Formula
end against horizontal surface: saw = 90° − a
end against vertical surface: saw = a

a = the brace’s angle above horizontal. Both ends together: (90° − a) + a = 90°.

Worked example

A brace that rises at 50° from horizontal.

  1. Top end, flat against the underside of the beam: 90° − 50° = 40°
  2. Bottom end, flat against the side of the post: 50°
  3. Check: 40° + 50° = 90° ✓

A 45° brace is the easy special case: 45° at both ends.

Common mistake

Cutting both brace ends at the same setting because they “look alike.” Unless the brace is exactly 45°, one end needs a and the other 90° − a. Hold the brace in place and mark both ends before cutting, or label them “post” and “beam.”

In geometry, a square cut makes a 90° angle with the board’s edge. A miter saw calls that same cut 0°, because its scale counts how far the blade has swung away from square. Bevel scales work the same way: 0° is a vertical blade.

So there are two ways to describe any cut: the geometric angle (between the cut line and the edge of the board) and the saw setting (between the cut line and a square line). They always add up to 90°.

Formula
saw setting = 90° − geometric angle
geometric angle = 90° − saw setting
Converting between the two scales
Worked example

A plan says “cut the end at 70° to the edge.”

  1. 90° − 70° = 20°

Set the miter saw to 20°. Setting it to 70° is impossible on most saws, which is often the hint that you have the wrong scale.

Common mistake

Reading an angle off a protractor (geometry) and dialing that number straight into the saw. Before setting the saw, ask: “Is 0 a square cut here?” If it is, you are on the saw scale.

A saw that is half a degree off looks perfect on a single cut. The trouble is that a frame adds up every cut. An octagon has 16 cut ends, and all their errors pile into the last joint you close.

Formula
total error = (number of cut ends) × (error per cut)
gap at the joint ≈ W × tan(total error)
Worked example
  1. Octagon, 16 cut ends, each 0.5° off: 16 × 0.5° = 8°
  2. Gap across a 3½″ joint: 3.5 × tan 8° = 0.49″, about ½″
  3. Square frame, 8 cut ends, each 0.5° off: 4° → 3.5 × tan 4° = 0.24″, about ¼″

To correct from a dry fit, divide the gap angle by the number of cut ends: an 8° gap on an octagon means each cut is 8° ÷ 16 = 0.5° off.

Test-cut routine
  • Cut two scrap pieces, put them together, and check the corner with a square. Any error you see is double the per-cut error.
  • Gap at the outside tips: the saw angle is too small. Gap at the inside (heel): too large.
  • For polygons, cut a full set of short test pieces and dry-fit the whole ring before cutting good wood.
Common mistake

Trusting the saw’s detents. Click-stops wear and can be off by half a degree from the factory. Check 90° and 45° with a combination square after any bump or blade change.

Look at the end of a mitered board from above. The cut line, the square line, and the edge of the board form a right triangle (a triangle with one 90° corner). The board’s width is one side. The distance you slide along the edge from the short point to the long point is the other side. Call it the offset.

The steeper the saw setting, the longer that slide. The tangent (tan) of an angle is simply the ratio “side across from the angle ÷ side next to it,” so it converts width into offset. Any phone calculator has a tan button; make sure it is in degrees (DEG), not radians.

Formula
offset = W × tan(θ)

W = width of the surface being cut across (3½″ on a 2×4 face). θ = saw setting (measured from square).

Worked example

2×4 face, W = 3.5″:

  1. 22.5°: 3.5 × 0.4142 = 1.45″ ≈ 1⁷⁄₁₆″
  2. 30°: 3.5 × 0.5774 = 2.02″ ≈ 2″
  3. 45°: 3.5 × 1.0000 = 3.50″ = 3½″
  4. 60°: 3.5 × 1.7321 = 6.06″ ≈ 6¹⁄₁₆″

Going from 45° to 60° almost doubles the offset. Offsets grow quickly at steep angles.

Common mistake

Using the geometric angle (60° to the edge) where the formula wants the saw setting (30° from square). That swaps tan 30° for tan 60° and triples the offset. If your answer is bigger than the board is wide at anything under 45°, you’ve used the wrong angle.

A mitered frame piece has two lengths: the outside length (long point to long point) and the inside length (short point to short point). The inside length is what you see around the opening, like the picture or the mirror. Each mitered end adds one offset, so a piece mitered at both ends is two offsets longer on the outside.

Formula
outside (LP–LP) = inside (SP–SP) + 2 × W × tan(θ)
inside = outside − 2 × W × tan(θ)

If the two ends are cut at different angles θ₁ and θ₂ (long points on the same edge), use W × tan θ₁ + W × tan θ₂ instead of 2 × W × tan θ.

Worked example

“I need a square frame whose opening is 24″ × 24″, from 2×4s laid flat.”

  1. Square frame → saw at 45°. Offset: 3.5 × tan 45° = 3.5″
  2. Long point length: 24 + 2 × 3.5 = 31″
  3. The frame’s outside size is also 31″ × 31″.

Cut four pieces at 31″ LP–LP. Second example, a hexagon with 12″ inside sides: offset 3.5 × tan 30° = 2.02″, so each piece is 12 + 4.04 = 16.04″ ≈ 16¹⁄₁₆″ LP–LP.

Common mistake

Cutting pieces to the opening size (24″) as if the corners were square. The frame then comes out 7″ too small inside. Decide first whether your number is an inside or an outside dimension, and write LP or SP next to it.

A bevel slopes through the thickness instead of across the face. The triangle is the same, it just stands on its edge. So the formula is identical; only W changes: use the thickness the blade passes through. For a 2×4 lying flat and beveled, that is 1½″.

Typical use: a four-sided box or planter frame where the corners are beveled so no end grain shows on the outside.

Formula
offset = T × tan(bevel)
outer face = inner face + 2 × T × tan(bevel)

T = thickness being cut through (1½″ for a 2×4, ¾″ for a 1×4).

Worked example

A square 2×4 frame (boards standing on edge) that must be 20″ across on the outside, corners beveled at 45°.

  1. Offset: 1.5 × tan 45° = 1.5″
  2. Outer face (long point to long point): 20″
  3. Inner face: 20 − 2 × 1.5 = 17″

An octagonal column wrap at 22.5° bevel on the same 2×4: offset 1.5 × 0.4142 = 0.62″ ≈ ⅝″ per end.

Common mistake

Using the face width (3½″) for a bevel. The blade passes through 1½″ of wood when beveling a flat 2×4, so the offset is much smaller. Look at what the blade actually travels through.

A handful of ratios cover most jobs. Memorise 45° and the “one-sixteenth per degree” rule and you can sanity-check almost any cut list in your head.

Rules of thumb
  • 45°: offset = width. A 3½″ board moves 3½″.
  • 30°: a bit over half the width (× 0.58). 2×4: about 2″.
  • 22.5°: a bit over two-fifths (× 0.41). 2×4: about 1⁷⁄₁₆″.
  • 15°: about a quarter (× 0.27). 2×4: about ¹⁵⁄₁₆″.
  • 60°: about 1¾ widths (× 1.73). 2×4: about 6″.
  • Small angles: each degree adds about W ÷ 57. On a 3½″ face that is almost exactly ¹⁄₁₆″ per degree, and it stays within ¹⁄₃₂″ up to about 20°.
Worked example

A 10° miter on a 2×4 face, no calculator.

  1. 10° × ¹⁄₁₆″ = ⅝″ (exact answer 0.617″)

Close enough to mark. For anything cut on a saw, you don’t need the offset at all: set the angle and measure the long-point length.

Common mistake

Stretching the per-degree rule to steep angles. At 45° it predicts 2.8″ instead of 3.5″. Past about 20°, switch to the named ratios above.

Builders often describe a slope by how much it climbs over a horizontal foot instead of in degrees. The climb is the rise, the horizontal distance is the run, and “6-in-12” pitch means 6″ of rise for every 12″ of run. On roof drawings it is shown as a small triangle with 12 on the flat side.

Pitch is easy to measure with a level and a tape, and it scales: a 6-in-12 roof rises 3″ over 6″ and 5 feet over 10 feet.

Formula
angle = arctan(rise ÷ 12)
rise per 12 = 12 × tan(angle)
sloped length = run × √(1 + (rise ÷ 12)²)

arctan (also written tan⁻¹ or atan) is the calculator button that turns a ratio back into an angle. The square-root factor is the “slope factor.”

Pitch to degrees
Worked example
  1. 6-in-12 to degrees: arctan(6 ÷ 12) = arctan(0.5) = 26.57°
  2. 30° to pitch: 12 × tan 30° = 6.93 → about 7-in-12
  3. Rafter over a 10 ft run at 6-in-12: 10 × √1.25 = 11.18 ft = 11′ 2³⁄₁₆″

Treating pitch as a percentage or as degrees. 6-in-12 is 50% grade and 26.57°, not 6° and not 60%. Always keep the “in-12”.

plumb-cut offset = W × rise ÷ 12

You need a compound cut when the boards meeting at a corner are tilted, not standing straight up or lying flat. Examples: a planter whose sides lean outward, a tray with sloped sides, a hopper, and crown molding, which sits tilted between wall and ceiling.

The surprise is that neither saw setting equals the angle you see. A four-sided planter still turns 90° at each corner, but because the sides lean, the joint line is no longer a simple 45° across the face. The miter and bevel have to share the job, and how they share it depends on the tilt.

Common mistake

Cutting a splayed box at 45° miter and 15° bevel because “it’s square with a 15° lean.” The corners will not close. Use the formulas below; the settings are 14.5° and 43.1° here.

See also

Two limits give the intuition. If the sides stand straight up (tilt 0°), a square box is cut with a pure 45° bevel. If the sides lie flat (tilt 90°), it is a picture frame with a pure 45° miter. For tilts in between, the miter grows from 0° toward 45° and the bevel shrinks from 45° toward 0°.

Formula
A = 180° ÷ n (or 90° − C ÷ 2 for any corner C)
miter = arctan( sin(S) × tan(A) )
bevel = arcsin( cos(S) × sin(A) )

S = tilt of the sides from vertical. A = the flat-frame saw setting for that corner. Board lies face-down on the saw table. For crown molding, S is the angle between the molding’s back and the wall: 38° for common “52/38” crown, 45° for “45/45” crown.

Worked example

Four-sided planter, sides tilted 15° from vertical.

  1. A = 180° ÷ 4 = 45°
  2. Miter: arctan(sin 15° × tan 45°) = arctan(0.2588 × 1) = 14.51°
  3. Bevel: arcsin(cos 15° × sin 45°) = arcsin(0.9659 × 0.7071) = arcsin(0.6830) = 43.08°

Set the saw to about 14.5° miter and 43° bevel. A 45° tilt gives the well-known pair 35.26° and 30°.

A taper is a board or leg that gets gradually narrower along its length, like a table leg that slims toward the floor. It is a long, shallow angled cut running with the grain instead of across it.

Describe a taper by how much wood comes off one face at the narrow end, over what length. From that you get the taper angle (what a jig or saw needs) and the taper per foot (how builders often specify it). Measure the width change in one direction: if only one face slopes in that direction, it loses the whole difference; if both opposite faces slope, each loses half.

Formula
removed per face = (wide − narrow) ÷ faces tapered
taper angle = arctan(removed ÷ taper length)
taper per foot = removed ÷ length × 12

Measure wide and narrow in one direction. “Faces tapered” is 1 if one face slopes in that direction, 2 if both opposite faces do. Table legs are usually tapered on the two inside faces, which are adjacent, so each direction has one sloping face.

Taper angle for material removed from one face
Worked example

Leg 1¾″ square at the top, 1⅜″ square at the floor, tapered on the two inside faces over 24″.

  1. Removed per face (one sloping face in each direction): (1.75 − 1.375) ÷ 1 = 0.375″ = ⅜″
  2. Angle: arctan(0.375 ÷ 24) = 0.90°
  3. Per foot: 0.375 ÷ 24 × 12 = 0.19″ (about ³⁄₁₆″ per foot)

Under one degree. That is why tapers are cut with a jig set by distance, not by reading a saw scale ().

Common mistake

Mixing up “per face” and “total”. A board 4″ wide tapered to 3″ on both edges loses ½″ per edge; take a full 1″ off each edge and it ends up 2″ wide.

Before any jig or saw, draw the taper on the wood. Two measurements define it: where the taper starts (often just below a table apron) and how much comes off at the end. A straight line between those two points is the cut line.

Lay out on the face you will cut, on every leg, with the legs labelled so the tapered faces all end up on the inside of the table.

  2. At the foot, measure in the amount to remove (⅜″) from the edge that will be cut.
  3. Join the two marks with a straightedge. Hatch the waste side.
  4. Repeat on the second tapered face, which is adjacent to the first on a table leg.
Worked example

A 29″ leg, apron 5″ deep, 24″ tapered, ⅜″ off each inside face.

  1. Start mark: 5″ from the top. Taper length: 29 − 5 = 24″.
  2. Foot mark: ⅜″ in from the inside edge.
  3. After the first face is cut, lay out the second face on the adjacent inside face.

Each saw has its own scales, its own strengths and its own way of hurting people. For every saw this guide covers the parts and scales, setup and calibration, the main cuts, capacity limits, blade choice, and safety. The table saw gets the longest section because it does the most and demands the most care.

Key points

  • Miter and bevel scales on miter saws read 0° at square. Table-saw miter gauges vary by brand: check yours with a square.
  • Table-saw kickback comes from wood touching the rising teeth at the back of the blade. The riving knife, a straight fence, push sticks and standing out of line prevent it.
  • Never use the table-saw fence as a length stop for crosscuts; use a stop block set before the blade.
  • A circular saw and a jigsaw cut on the upstroke: put the good face down. A table saw and miter saw cut downward: good face up.
  • Check every saw with a test cut: flip one piece and any error shows double.

A miter saw (chop saw) is a circular blade on a hinged arm that you pull down through a board held against a fence. The arm sits on a turntable that swings left and right for miters, and it tilts for bevels. A sliding miter saw also slides forward and back on rails, so it can cut much wider boards.

  • Miter scale: on the front of the turntable, 0° = square cut. Click-stops (detents) usually sit at 0°, 15°, 22.5°, 31.6° (for crown molding), and 45°, both sides.
  • Bevel scale: behind the blade, 0° = blade vertical. Many saws tilt one way only; a dual-bevel saw tilts both ways.
  • Kerf slot: the slot in the table insert shows exactly where the blade exits. Line your mark up with its edge.
Worked example

Setting 24.5° (between detents) for the out-of-square bay corner in .

  1. Squeeze the detent override so the turntable moves freely.
  2. Swing to 24.5° on the scale, lock the miter handle.
  3. Make a test cut on scrap and check it against the corner.
Safety
  • Keep hands out of the marked no-hands zone, usually about 6″ each side of the blade.
  • Wood must be flat on the table and tight to the fence; never cut freehand.
  • Let the blade reach full speed before cutting and stop completely before lifting it.
Common mistake

Locking the miter handle after the turntable has crept off the number. Read the scale again after locking; many saws shift a few tenths when the lock bites.

A miter saw is safe when three things are true: your hands are well away from the blade’s path, the wood can’t move, and the offcut has somewhere to go. Most accidents come from holding short pieces with fingers near the blade, reaching across the blade, or grabbing offcuts while it is still spinning.

Safety rules
  • No-hands zone: about 6″ either side of the blade, and never cross your arms over its path.
  • Clamp any piece too short to hold outside the zone, or cut it from a longer board.
  • Support long boards level with the table at both ends so they don’t tip and pinch the blade.
  • Let the blade stop before lifting it or reaching for an offcut.
  • Unplug before changing blades or adjusting under the guard.
  • Wear eye and hearing protection.
Worked example

You need a 3″-long block with 45° ends.

  1. Don’t cut a 3″ scrap. Miter the end of a 2-foot board first.
  2. Measure 3″, clamp the board, cut the second miter. The block falls free; the hand holding the long board stays a foot away.
Common mistake

Pulling a sliding saw toward you through the wood. Pull the head out first, lower it, then push it back through the cut. Pulling into the wood lets the blade climb toward you.

Every miter saw has a maximum width it can cut, and that width shrinks when you swing the saw. The angled cut line is longer than the board is wide (it is width ÷ cos(miter)), but the blade can only travel so far. A bevel reduces the height the saw can cut through.

Your manual has a capacity chart. As a rough guide, a 10″ non-sliding saw cuts a 2×6 square but only a 2×4 at a 45° miter.

Formula (rough)
width capacity at miter m ≈ capacity at 0° × cos(m)
Worked example

A saw that crosscuts 5½″ at 0°:

  1. At 45°: 5.5 × 0.707 = 3.9″
  2. At 22.5°: 5.5 × 0.924 = 5.1″

A 2×4 (3½″) fits at 45°; a 2×6 (5½″) does not.

Boards wider than capacity

  • Use a circular saw with a speed square or straightedge ().
  • Use a table saw with a crosscut sled ().
Common mistake

Forcing a board that is just too wide. The blade stops short of the back edge and leaves a sliver, and people then try to finish it by lifting the board into the blade. Check the capacity before you start a cut.

  • Flip the board: keep the saw at 45° one way and turn the board over. It saves resetting the saw, but only works for plain square-edged stock.
Worked example

A picture frame from molding with a rabbet on the back.

  1. Face up, rabbet edge against the fence, for every cut.
  2. End 1: saw at 45° right. Measure the LP length. End 2: saw at 45° left.

Flipping profiled molding to get the second angle. The profile ends up upside down on one end, and the corners won’t match. Swing the saw instead.

On a table saw the blade sticks up through a flat table and you push the wood into it. The blade stays put; the wood moves. That makes it the best tool for long straight rips and accurate repeat cuts, and it is why guiding the wood safely matters so much.

  • Rip fence: the long guide parallel to the blade that sets the width of a rip.
  • Miter slots and miter gauge: the gauge slides in a slot to push wood across the blade for crosscuts.
  • Throat plate: the insert around the blade.
  • Riving knife: a curved steel fin just behind the blade that rises and falls with it and keeps the kerf from closing on the blade. Some saws use a fixed splitter instead.
  • Blade guard and anti-kickback pawls: the clear cover over the blade and the toothed fingers that dig in if wood moves backward.
  • Height and tilt wheels: raise the blade and tilt it for bevels.
Worked example

Which part does each cut use?

  1. Ripping a 2×6 down to 3½″: rip fence set 3½″ from the blade.
  2. Cutting a 1×4 to 18″ long: miter gauge or crosscut sled, never the fence.
  3. A 45° bevel rip: tilt wheel, fence on the side the blade tilts away from.
Safety
  • Keep the riving knife installed for every through-cut.
  • Read the next topic, on kickback, before your first cut.
Common mistake

Removing the riving knife “because it gets in the way” and never putting it back. It is the single most effective protection against kickback.

Kickback is when the saw grabs the wood and throws it back at the operator, often hard enough to injure, and sometimes pulls the operator’s hand toward the blade as it goes.

It happens at the back of the blade. Up front, the teeth move down and toward you, pressing the wood onto the table. At the back, the teeth rise up and come toward you. If the wood touches those rising teeth (because the kerf closes, the board twists away from the fence, or an offcut gets trapped), they lift it and fire it forward.

What prevents kickback
  • Riving knife in place, so the kerf can’t close on the rear teeth.
  • Fence parallel to the blade, or the back end toed out slightly (about ¹⁄₆₄″), never toward the blade.
  • Stand to the side of the blade line, never directly behind it.
  • Push the wood all the way past the blade with a push stick, keeping it tight to the fence.
  • Never use the fence as a stop for crosscuts, and never cut freehand.
  • Don’t reach over the blade; let the cut piece go past, turn the saw off, then retrieve it.
Worked example

Ripping a 2×4 that has been drying in the sun. Halfway through, the kerf starts to close behind the blade (the wood had built-up stress).

  1. With a riving knife: the knife holds the kerf open; the cut finishes.
  2. Without one: the pinched wood rides up the rear teeth and kicks back.
Common mistake

Standing directly behind the board to push hard. If it kicks back, it comes straight at you. Stand beside the line and push with a stick.

Three simple things keep your hands far from the blade and the wood under control:

  • Push stick/push block: a notched stick or a handle with a grippy base that pushes the wood the last part of the way. Use one whenever your hand would come within about 6″ of the blade (any rip narrower than about 6″).
  • Featherboard: a board with flexible fingers, clamped to the table or fence, that keeps the wood pressed against the fence. Place it ahead of the blade, never beside or behind it, or it will pinch the kerf.
  • Blade height: set the teeth to clear the top of the wood by about ⅛″–¼″. Less blade above the wood is less blade to touch.
Worked example

Ripping a 1×4 (¾″ thick) to 2½″ wide.

  1. Blade height: ¾ + ¼ = 1″ above the table.
  2. Fence at 2½″. Featherboard on the table before the blade, pressing toward the fence.
  3. Feed by hand until the back end reaches the table edge, then finish with the push stick between the blade and fence.
Safety
  • Keep a push stick on the saw at all times so it is always within reach.
  • Push sticks are cheap and made to be cut. Replace them when chewed up.
Common mistake

A featherboard placed level with or past the blade. It squeezes the kerf shut on the rear teeth, the exact condition that causes kickback.

A table saw is accurate only if three things line up: the blade parallel to the miter slots, the fence parallel to the blade, and the blade square to the table at 0° tilt (and at 45° when tilted). Check them when the saw is new, after moving it, and whenever cuts come out wrong.

  1. Blade to slot: unplug. Mark one tooth. Measure from the slot to that tooth at the front, rotate the blade by hand to bring the tooth to the back, measure again. The two should match within a few thousandths.
  2. Fence to blade: measure from the fence to the same slot at front and back. Parallel, or the back end a hair (≈ ¹⁄₆₄″) further from the blade.
  3. Tilt: raise the blade fully. Zero a digital gauge on the table, stick it on the blade, set 0.0° and 45.0°, and adjust the tilt stops.
Worked example

Slot-to-tooth reads 5.235″ at the front and 5.250″ at the back.

  1. Difference: 0.015″ over the tooth-to-tooth distance (about 8″ on a 10″ blade).
  2. Loosen the trunnion bolts (per the manual) and tap the back of the trunnion toward the slot until the readings match.

Cuts stop burning on one side, and crosscuts come out square.

Common mistake

Aligning the fence to the blade before the blade is aligned to the slot. Always blade first, then fence, then any sleds and jigs that ride in the slot.

A rip on the table saw runs the board along the fence, so the width comes out even along the whole length. The piece between the blade and the fence is normally the keeper.

For thin strips (under about ½″), don’t cut them between the blade and fence, where they can be trapped and thrown. Cut them on the far side of the blade instead, using a thin-rip jig in the miter slot to set the width, and move the fence over for each strip.

Worked example

Ripping a 2×6 (5½″) into two equal pieces with a ⅛″ kerf.

  1. Each piece: (5.5 − 0.125) ÷ 2 = 2.6875″ = 2¹¹⁄₁₆″
  2. Set the fence 2¹¹⁄₁₆″ from the blade (measure to a tooth set toward the fence).

Both pieces come out 2¹¹⁄₁₆″ wide.

Safety
  • The board’s edge against the fence must be straight. Joint or rip a crooked edge straight first.
  • Use a push stick for the last 12″, and keep pushing until the wood is fully past the riving knife.
  • Support long boards at the outfeed end so they don’t tip up off the table.
Common mistake

Ripping a board with a curved (crowned) edge against the fence. The board rocks, the cut wanders, and it can bind. Put a straight edge on the fence, or snap a line and cut it straight with a circular saw first.

Tilt the blade and rip along the fence to cut a bevel along a board’s length (a bevel rip), or just clip the corner (a chamfer). The rule for safety: the blade should tilt away from the fence. If it tilts toward the fence, the piece between the blade and the fence ends up underneath the tilted blade and boxed in by the fence, with nowhere to go but back at you.

Most saws tilt left (left-tilt), so the fence stays on the right. On a right-tilt saw, move the fence to the left side of the blade for bevel rips.

Beveling both edges of ¾″ stock at 22.5° for an octagonal column wrap.

  • Blade tilts away from the fence. Re-check the riving knife clears at full tilt.

Setting the fence distance with the blade vertical, then tilting. The tilt moves where the blade meets the table. Tilt first, then set the fence and make a test cut.

For crosscuts, the wood rides on the miter gauge, which slides in a miter slot. The fence stays out of the way. An auxiliary wooden face screwed to the gauge (and extending past the blade) supports the wood right up to the cut and holds a stop block.

Check the gauge’s scale. Some read 0° for a square cut, like a miter saw; others read 90° for square, like a protractor. The only way to know is to set it to its “square” mark and check against a square.

Worked example

You need a 30° saw setting (60° to the edge) and your gauge reads 90° at square.

  1. Its scale reads geometry: set it to 90 − 30 = 60°.
  2. On a gauge that reads 0° at square, you would set 30°.
Safety
  • Never use the rip fence as a length stop for a crosscut: the offcut gets trapped between blade and fence and is thrown. Clamp a stop block to the fence well in front of the blade instead, so the piece is free by the time it reaches the blade.
  • Hold the wood firmly to the gauge face; clamp it for angled cuts, where it tends to creep.

Trusting the gauge’s detents for picture frames. Gauges often have slop in the slot. For frames, a miter sled is much more accurate ().

A crosscut sled is a plywood base with runners in both miter slots and a fence square to the blade. The wood sits still on the sled while the whole sled slides, so it cuts wide boards square and safely.

The sled is only as good as its fence. The 5-cut method measures fence error very precisely: cut all four edges of a square panel in turn (rotating it the same way each time), then cut a thin strip off the first edge. Any error builds up four times, so the strip tapers by four times the error.

Formula
error per cut = (A − B) ÷ 4, over the strip length L
move the fence end = (A − B) ÷ 4 × fence length ÷ L

A and B = strip width at each end (use calipers). The fence pivots about one end.

Worked example

18″ square panel. The strip measures 0.265″ at one end and 0.253″ at the other. Fence is 24″ long.

  1. A − B = 0.012″
  2. Error per cut: 0.012 ÷ 4 = 0.003″ over 18″ (0.0095°)
  3. Move the fence end: 0.003 × 24 ÷ 18 = 0.004″

Four thousandths of an inch: a sheet of paper. Tap the fence, re-tighten, and repeat the test until the difference is under 0.004″.

Safety
  • Put a thick block on the back of the sled fence where the blade exits, so the blade never emerges from the fence.
  • Keep hands behind the fence line and out of the blade path; add a stop so the sled can’t be pushed far enough to expose the blade.
Common mistake

Rotating the panel the wrong way between cuts, or flipping it over. Keep the same face up and turn it the same direction every time, or the errors cancel instead of adding.

A miter sled has two fences at 45° to the blade and exactly 90° to each other, forming a V. Cut one end of a frame piece against the left fence and the mating end against the right fence. Because the fences are 90° apart, the two cuts always add up to 90°, even if each is slightly off 45°.

Worked example

The fences are 44.8° and 45.2° to the blade, but exactly 90° to each other.

  1. Mating ends: 44.8 + 45.2 = 90°
  2. Every corner closes square; only the joint line leans a tiny 0.2° from the diagonal.
Safety
  • Clamp short pieces to the fence; your hands stay outside the V.
Common mistake

Cutting both ends of every piece on the same fence. Then errors add instead of cancel. Always cut mating ends on opposite fences.

Tapers run at angles far too shallow to set on a scale, so a taper jig sets them by distance. The common kind is two boards hinged at one end, with an adjustable stop that holds them apart. The jig rides against the fence; the leg rides against the jig. Opening the jig by a measured gap at a known distance from the hinge sets the taper angle.

Formula
gap = distance from hinge × tan(taper angle)
or: gap at the leg’s length = material removed
Worked example

The leg from : ⅜″ off over 24″ (0.895°).

  1. Gap at 12″ from the hinge: 12 × tan 0.895° = 0.1875″ = ³⁄₁₆″
  2. Set the fence so the blade meets the leg exactly at the start mark.
  3. Cut the first inside face. Turn the leg 90° to the second inside face and cut again with the same setting.

For tapers on two opposite faces, the second cut needs double the gap, because the first tapered face now lies against the jig.

Safety
  • Clamp the leg to the jig (toggle clamps are ideal) so it can’t twist into the blade.
  • Push the jig, not the leg, and keep going until both are past the riving knife.
Common mistake

Measuring the gap at the far end of a jig that is longer than the leg and forgetting to scale. Measure the gap at the distance you calculated for, or recompute for where you measure.

Not every table-saw cut goes all the way through. Three flat-bottomed slots are common in cabinets and boxes:

Worked example

A ¼″-deep dado for a ¾″ shelf, cut with a ⅛″ blade.

  1. Blade height ¼″.
  2. Width ¾″: ¾ ÷ ⅛ = 6 overlapping passes, moving the stop ⅛″ each time (or one pass with a ¾″ dado stack).

Cabinet sides 23¼″ deep from a 48″-wide sheet.

The circular saw is the framer’s workhorse: you move the saw, not the wood, so it cuts anything you can reach, from 2×12s to full sheets. Its blade spins so the teeth come up through the wood from underneath, which means splinters appear on the top face. Cut with the good face down.

  • Depth: set the blade about ¼″ below the bottom of the board. Less blade exposed, less kickback, cleaner cut.
  • Bevel: the base plate (shoe) tilts from 0° to 45° or more; the scale reads 0° with the blade square to the base.
  • Sight line: a notch at the front of the base marks the blade line. Check it against a test cut; it’s often slightly off.
Worked example

Cutting a 2×10 (1½″ thick).

  1. Depth: 1½ + ¼ = 1¾″.
  2. Unplugged, hold the saw beside the board edge and set the depth so the teeth hang ¼″ below.
Safety
  • Two hands on the saw; the blade guard must snap back freely.
  • Stand to the side of the cut line, not behind the saw.
  • Let the blade stop before lifting the saw out of the kerf.
Common mistake

Leaving the blade at full depth for thin stock. Several inches of exposed teeth under the board catch clamps, cords and bench tops, and make kickback violent.

For square and angled crosscuts on framing lumber, hold a speed square’s lip against the far edge of the board and run the saw’s base plate along the square’s edge. It is fast, accurate enough for framing, and works at any angle the square can pivot to.

  2. Slide the square up against the base plate’s edge; hook its lip on the board edge; hold it firmly.
  3. Cut, keeping the base plate against the square the whole way.
Worked example

A 22.5° miter on a 2×6: pivot the square to 22.5 on its degree scale (), hold it, and guide the saw along it.

  • Keep the hand holding the square behind it, well away from the blade path.

Lining up the square with the pencil line instead of the saw. The square guides the base plate, which is offset from the blade. Set the blade on the line first, then bring the square to the plate.

Worked example

Cutting 2 feet off an 8-foot 2×6 on sawhorses.

  2. Cut so the 2-foot offcut overhangs and drops away.

  • If the saw starts to bind, release the trigger and hold the saw still until it stops. Don’t back it out while spinning.
Common mistake

Supporting both ends of a long offcut and cutting in the middle. Both halves sag toward the cut and pinch the blade.

  • Keep the base flat on the work and the blade clear of the bench, clamps and cords underneath.
Common mistake

Turning the saw too sharply. The blade twists, the cut goes out of square and the blade may snap. If it won’t follow, back out and add relief cuts.

A coping saw holds a very thin blade in a C-shaped frame. The blade can be turned in the frame, so it follows tight curves, and it is the tool for coping trim joints (). Most people mount the blade with the teeth pointing toward the handle so it cuts on the pull stroke.

Worked example

Coping a baseboard with a 15–20 teeth-per-inch blade: tension the blade until it rings when plucked, tilt the saw to back-cut about 30°, and follow the profile line.

Common mistake

A slack blade. It wanders and snaps. Tighten the handle until the blade is taut.

Trim is where angled cuts are most visible and walls are least square. This guide covers which way each corner goes, how to measure between corners, coping (the carpenter’s answer to crooked inside corners), the finishing details of baseboard and casing, and crown molding.

Key points

  • Inside corner: long point on the back (wall side). Outside corner: long point on the front.
  • Cope inside corners, miter outside corners.
  • Scarf long runs over a stud with matching bevels; the steeper the bevel, the longer the overlap.
  • Crown nested upside down and backwards: miter only, 90° − corner ÷ 2. Crown flat: use the compound table.

At an inside corner the two pieces of trim meet with their backs reaching all the way into the corner, so the long point is on the back (the wall side) and the front face is shorter. At an outside corner the trim wraps around the wall, so the long point is on the front and the back stops at the wall’s corner.

This is the most common trim mistake: cutting a perfect 45° in the wrong direction. Before cutting, hold the board in place and say “long point on the wall” or “long point in the room.”

Rule
inside corner: front = back − t × tan(saw)
outside corner: front = back + t × tan(saw)

t = trim thickness. At a 90° corner (saw 45°) the front is shorter or longer than the back by exactly t.

Worked example

⅝″-thick baseboard, square corners.

  1. Inside corner: the front stops ⅝″ short of the corner.
  2. Outside corner: the front sticks out ⅝″ past the wall’s corner.
Common mistake

Measuring the front face of a piece that runs between two inside corners. Measure wall to wall; that is the back length, and the saw makes the front shorter on its own.

See also

Measure trim lengths along the wall, at the height the trim will sit, and write down which kind of corner is at each end. The measurement is the length of the back of the trim; the saw produces the front.

  • Inside to inside: wall to wall. Both ends long point at the back.
  • Inside to outside: from the inside corner to the outside corner of the wall. Mark that length on the back; the outside end’s front long point is t longer.
  • Outside to outside: the wall’s length between its two outside corners, on the back.

For outside corners, the most reliable method is to skip the tape: hold the board in place and mark the wall corner on its back edge directly.

Worked example

Wall 96″ from an inside corner to an outside corner; baseboard ⅝″ thick.

  1. Back length: 96″.
  2. Front at the inside end: ⅝″ short of the corner. Front at the outside end: 96⅝″ from the inside corner.
  3. Cut the board a hair long (¹⁄₁₆″), test, and trim.
Common mistake

Measuring at the floor, where the wall flares out with drywall mud. Measure at the height of the top of the baseboard.

See also

A mitered inside corner only closes if the corner is exactly the angle you cut. Real inside corners are rarely 90°, and when they are off, the two faces of the miter pull apart and a gap shows right at the front. Wood movement makes it worse over time.

A coped joint avoids this. The first piece runs square into the corner. The second piece has its end cut to the exact shape of the first piece’s profile, so it slides over the face of the first piece like a jigsaw piece. If the corner is a degree or two off, the coped piece just sits at a slightly different angle, and the joint still looks tight.

Worked example

A 93° corner with 3½″ baseboard.

  1. Mitered at 45° each: each piece is 1.5° off, and the gap at the front grows to about 3.5 × tan 3° ≈ ³⁄₁₆″ across the joint.
  2. Coped: the second piece simply meets the first at 93°; no gap.
Common mistake

Coping outside corners. Coping only works at inside corners, where one piece can slide over the other. Outside corners are always mitered.

See also

Coping takes a few tries to learn and then becomes quicker than fussing with miters. You need a miter saw, a coping saw with a fine blade, and a file or rasp.

  1. Install the first piece square-cut, tight into the corner.
  2. On the second piece, cut a 45° inside miter (long point at the back) as if you were mitering the corner. The line where the cut meets the face is the profile line. Rub a pencil along it so it shows.
  3. With the coping saw, cut along the profile line, tilting the saw about 30° so you remove wood from behind the line (a back-cut). Only the thin front edge should remain at the line.
  4. For the flat top part of the profile, cut straight (square) so the top edge stays crisp.
  5. Test-fit against a scrap of the same molding. File or rasp high spots until it closes.
Worked example

Finding the length of the coped piece.

  1. Measure wall to wall and mark that length on the back of the second piece.
  2. Make the 45° reveal cut with its long point on that mark.
  3. After coping, the front edge lands exactly on the face of the first piece, ⅝″ out from the wall for ⅝″ baseboard.

Cut ¹⁄₁₆″ long, test, and trim to fit.

Common mistake

Coping straight (no back-cut). Then the whole end has to match the first piece exactly, and any bump holds the joint open. Back-cut generously; the front edge does all the work.

See also

With coped inside corners, order matters: each coped piece has to go in after the piece it fits over. A good plan:

  1. Start on the wall opposite the door with a piece square-cut at both ends, tight into both corners.
  2. Work around the room so each new piece is coped on the end that meets the previous piece, and square or mitered at the other end.
  3. Miter outside corners; install mitered pairs together.
  4. Save the least visible joint (often behind the door) for last: the last piece may need coping at both ends.
Worked example

The room in the figure, with the door in the bottom wall.

  1. Piece 1 on the top wall, square at both ends (S–S).
  2. Pieces 2 and 3 on the side walls: coped at the top where they meet piece 1, square into the bottom corners (C–S).
  3. Pieces 4 and 5 on the bottom wall: coped into the corners, square against the door casing (C–S).

Every inside corner has one square piece and one coped piece. An outside corner anywhere along the way would be a mitered pair (M).

Common mistake

Starting beside the door and ending opposite it, where everyone looks first. Put the tricky double-coped piece where it will be least noticed.

When a wall is longer than your trim stock, join two pieces end to end with a scarf joint: both ends cut at the same bevel so they overlap, instead of butting square. A scarf hides the seam better, and when the wood shrinks it shows as a thin line rather than a gap.

Put the joint over a stud so both pieces can be nailed, glue the bevels, and overlap so the seam faces away from the main view (the front piece laps over the back one).

Formula
overlap = t × tan(bevel)

t = trim thickness. A steeper bevel gives more overlap and a stronger, less visible joint.

Worked example

⅝″ baseboard, 45° bevel.

  1. Overlap: 0.625 × tan 45° = ⅝″
  2. Both pieces measured to the long point of their bevel; both land on the stud at 64″ from the corner.
Common mistake

Cutting one bevel at 45° left and the other at 45° left too. The two bevels must be opposite hands (one piece’s bevel faces the wall, the other faces the room) so they lap. Dry-fit before nailing.

Where trim ends in open space (a baseboard stopping at an opening, a chair rail, a window stool), a square end shows the rough end grain and the back of the profile. A mitered return turns the profile back into the wall: miter the end like an outside corner, and glue on a tiny piece with a matching miter and a square back.

Worked example

A ⅝″-thick baseboard return.

  1. Miter the end of the baseboard at 45°, long point on the front.
  2. Miter the end of a long scrap of the same molding the opposite way.
  3. Cut the return off that scrap with a square cut at ⅝″ from the long point (the depth to the wall).
  4. Glue it on; hold with tape until set.

  • Never try to hold a ⅝″ piece near a miter-saw blade. Cut it off the end of a long board, and let the blade stop before retrieving it (or use a hand saw).
Common mistake

Nailing the return. It splits. Glue it (with tape as a clamp) or use a single pin nail from the back.

Casing is the trim around a door or window. It doesn’t sit flush with the edge of the jamb (the frame the door hangs in); it steps back by a small reveal, usually ³⁄₁₆″. The reveal hides small wobbles in the jamb edge and looks deliberate.

Mark the reveal on the jamb edges with a combination square set to ³⁄₁₆″. Where the side and head reveal lines cross at each top corner is the short point of the miter.

Formula
head SP–SP = opening width + 2 × reveal
head LP–LP = head SP–SP + 2 × casing width
Worked example

Opening 30″ between jambs, ³⁄₁₆″ reveal, 2½″ casing.

  1. Head short points: 30 + 2 × ³⁄₁₆ = 30⅜″
  2. Head long points: 30⅜ + 2 × 2½ = 35⅜″
  3. Legs: measure from the floor to the head reveal line at each side (floors are rarely level), long point at the top.
Common mistake

Measuring one leg and cutting both the same. Floors slope; measure each side separately.

Crown molding bridges the corner between wall and ceiling at an angle, with a hollow behind it. Two flat strips on its back (the flats) rest on the wall and the ceiling. The angle it sits at is the spring angle, and the common kinds are named for it:

  • 52/38 crown: its back makes 52° with the ceiling and 38° with the wall.
  • 45/45 crown: 45° to each.

To check an unknown crown, set it in the corner of a framing square exactly as it sits on the wall and measure the angles.

Worked example

A crown held in a framing square reaches 3½″ down the wall and 2¾″ out along the ceiling.

  1. Angle from the wall: arctan(2.75 ÷ 3.5) = 38.2°
  2. Angle from the ceiling: 90 − 38.2 = 51.8°

It is 52/38 crown: use S = 38° in the flat-cut formula.

Common mistake

Using the 52/38 settings on 45/45 crown. The corners won’t close. Measure the spring angle of every new batch of molding.

A perfectly cut miter still has to be held together. Miters glue end grain to end grain, which is the weakest glue joint in woodworking, so they need help. This short guide covers how to glue, reinforce, fasten and clamp angled joints, and why wide miters open with the seasons.

Key points

  • Size end grain with a first coat of glue, then glue again.
  • A glued miter alone is weak; add a spline, key, biscuit, dowel or screw.
  • Dry-fit and clamp everything before glue goes on.
  • Wide solid-wood miters open at the inside corner when the wood shrinks.

Wood glue is strongest on long grain, where the fibres lie side by side. A miter face is mostly end grain: open fibre tips that wick glue away like a bundle of straws, leaving a dry, starved joint.

The fix is to size the end grain: brush on a thin first coat of glue, let it soak in for a few minutes, then apply a normal coat and clamp.

  1. Dry-fit and set up the clamps first.
  2. Brush a thin coat on both miter faces. Wait 3–5 minutes.
  3. Brush a second, normal coat. Assemble, clamp, and wipe squeeze-out with a damp rag.
Worked example

A picture frame glued with a single coat versus a sized joint.

  1. Single coat: the faces look dry within minutes; the joint breaks with light hand pressure after curing.
  2. Sized: a thin glue line stays wet; the joint is noticeably stronger, though still weaker than a reinforced one.
Common mistake

Relying on glue alone for anything that carries a load. A glued miter is fine for a small picture frame; a door frame or box that gets handled needs reinforcement ().

See also

Reinforcements bridge the joint with long grain or metal:

  • Spline: a thin strip of wood glued into matching slots cut in both miter faces. Invisible if stopped short of the edge.
  • Key (or feather): a slot cut across the outside corner after assembly, filled with a thin slip of wood, often a contrasting colour.
  • Biscuit: an oval wafer of compressed wood in slots cut by a biscuit joiner.
  • Dowels: round pins across the joint, in drilled holes.
  • Pocket screws: angled screws from the back of a frame, hidden when it hangs on the wall.
Worked example

Keys for a ¾″ × 2½″ frame, cut on the table saw with a 45° cradle jig.

  1. Blade height about ⅝″, so the slot reaches well across the joint line.
  2. Two slots per corner, ½″ in from each face. Glue in ⅛″ slips, flush-trim when dry.
Common mistake

Cutting spline slots too deep toward the inside corner so they break through. Stop the slot at least ¼″ from the inside edge, or run it through deliberately and plan for it to show.

Trim miters are held mainly by nails into the wall, but the corner itself needs fixing too, or it will open as the house moves. Glue the miter faces, then drive a thin brad or pin nail through the corner from each side so the nails cross the joint.

Worked example

Door casing head joint: glue the miter, then one 1¼″ brad down through the top of the head into the leg, and one from the side of the leg into the head.

Common mistake

Nailing too close to the tip of the long point. It splits. Keep brads at least ¾″ back from the point, and angle them slightly away from it.

Miters slide under clamp pressure because the faces are at 45° to the force. Three ways to hold them closed:

  • Band clamp: a strap around the whole frame with corner blocks, tightened with a ratchet. It pulls all four corners closed at once.
  • Corner clamps: hold one corner at 90° while the glue sets.
  • Tape trick: for small boxes, lay the sides face down end to end, tape across the joints, flip, glue, and roll them up. The tape is the hinge and the clamp.
Worked example

A 12″ square frame with a band clamp.

  1. Dry-fit with the band on. Check the diagonals ().
  2. Glue, re-clamp, check diagonals again. Push the long diagonal’s corners together if needed.
Common mistake

Over-tightening, which squeezes out all the glue and can bow the frame. Snug is enough; the corners should close with a thin glue line.

Wood shrinks across its width but not its length (). In a mitered corner, the grain of the two pieces runs in different directions, so as each piece narrows, the angle of its miter face changes slightly. When the wood dries and shrinks, the joint opens at the inside corner (the heel); when it swells, it opens at the outside tips.

Narrow miters (casing, picture frames) move too little to notice. Wide ones (a mitered tabletop edge, a box made from wide boards) can open a visible gap.

Formula
gap at the heel ≈ 1.4 × change in width

1.4 ≈ √2 for a 45° miter. Change in width from .

Worked example

A mitered 1×8 (7¼″ wide), C = 0.003, moisture drops 4 points.

  1. Width change: 7.25 × 0.003 × 4 = 0.087″
  2. Heel gap: 1.41 × 0.087 = 0.12″ ≈ ⅛″

Visible. Use plywood or MDF for wide mitered parts, keep solid-wood miters under about 4″, use quartersawn stock, or choose a different joint.

Common mistake

Cutting and gluing wide miters in a humid shop and bringing them into a heated house. Let the wood acclimatize in the room where it will live for a week or two before final cuts.

Framing is where all the earlier guides come together at full scale: tape and square, pitch and plumb cuts, and a lot of repeated 2× cuts. Code numbers here are from the National Building Code – 2023 Alberta Edition (Part 9, housing), which applies in Calgary. The code is written in millimetres; the inch values are conversions rounded to the safe side. The City of Calgary issues permits and has the final word, and structural sizes (headers, joists, rafters) come from the code’s span tables or your plans, not from this guide.

Key points

  • 16″ on center: first layout line at 15¼″, then every 16″. Sheet edges land on stud centers.
  • 92⅝″ precut studs + three 1½″ plates = a 97⅛″ wall, right for an 8-foot ceiling.
  • Header length = rough opening + 3″.
  • Rafter: run = span ÷ 2 − ridge ÷ 2; length = run × slope factor. Plumb cut = slope angle.
  • Stairs: risers = total rise ÷ 7½, rounded up; treads = risers − 1; drop the stringer by one tread thickness.

Code values quoted are from the National Building Code – 2023 Alberta Edition, in force in Calgary since May 1, 2024. Check with the City of Calgary before building; permits are required for most structural work.

Walls are laid out on their plates before any stud is cut. Lay the bottom plate and the top plate side by side, edges flush and ends aligned, and mark both at once (ganging the plates). Then every stud lands in the same place top and bottom.

Framers use a small set of marks: a line on one side of the stud position and a letter on the side the stud goes:

  • X: common stud. K: king stud. J: jack (trimmer) stud. C: cripple.
  • Mark the crown of every stud and joist and install them crown-the-same-way ().
Worked example

A wall with a window between the 3rd and 5th stud positions.

  1. Mark the regular layout (X) along the full plate first.
  2. Mark the window’s king (K) and jack (J) studs from the rough opening, and change any X that falls inside the opening to C (cripple).
Common mistake

Putting the X on the wrong side of the line. The line marks the stud’s edge; the X shows which side the 1½″ of stud occupies. Be consistent along the whole plate.

See also

On center (OC) spacing measures from the center of one stud to the center of the next: usually 16″ (walls, floors) or 24″. Sheets of plywood, OSB and drywall are 48″ wide, so with 16″ or 24″ layout their edges fall on the center of a stud, where they can be nailed.

The trick: hook the tape on the end of the plate and make the first line at 15¼″, with the X past it. That puts the stud’s center at 16″. Every following line is 16″ further (31¼, 47¼…), which is why tapes print the 16″ marks in red.

Layout lines from the end of the plate
Formula
layout line n = n × OC − ¾″
studs ≈ wall length ÷ OC, rounded up, + 1

Plus extra studs at corners, intersections and openings.

Worked example

A 12-foot (144″) wall at 16″ OC.

  1. Lines at 15¼, 31¼, 47¼, 63¼, 79¼, 95¼, 111¼, 127¼. End stud flush at 142½–144.
  2. Studs: 144 ÷ 16 = 9, + 1 = 10 (the first at the end, eight on layout, one at the far end).
Common mistake

Marking the first stud at 16″. Then its center is at 16¾″ and every sheet edge misses the studs by ¾″, leaving nothing to nail to.

A stud wall is a ladder of vertical studs between a bottom plate (sole plate) and a doubled top plate. Openings need a few special pieces:

  • Header: a beam over the opening that carries the load above it.
  • King stud: full-height stud on each side of the opening.
  • Jack stud (trimmer): shorter stud nailed to the king that holds up the header.
  • Sill: the horizontal piece at the bottom of a window opening.
  • Cripples: short studs above the header and below the sill, on layout.
Worked example

Count the pieces for one window in a 16″ OC wall: 2 kings, 2 jacks, 1 header, 1 sill, cripples above and below wherever a layout mark falls inside the opening.

Common mistake

Leaving out the jack studs and nailing the header to the kings. Nails alone won’t carry the load; the header must bear on jacks.

See also

Wall height is the stud length plus the plates. For 8-foot ceilings, lumber yards sell precut studs at 92⅝″: add a 1½″ bottom plate and a doubled 1½″ top plate and the wall is 97⅛″, which leaves room for 96″ drywall sheets plus the ceiling drywall.

Formula
wall height = stud + 1½″ × number of plates
Worked example
  1. 8-foot ceiling: 92⅝ + 3 × 1½ = 97⅛″
  2. 9-foot ceiling with 104⅝″ precuts: 104⅝ + 4½ = 109⅛″
  3. A custom 100″ wall: stud = 100 − 4½ = 95½″
Common mistake

Cutting studs to 96″ for an “8-foot wall”. The wall ends up 100½″ tall and the drywall comes up short at the top.

A rough opening (RO) is the framed hole a door or window goes into, a little bigger than the unit so it can be shimmed plumb. The manufacturer gives the RO; for a typical prehung interior door it is the door width + 2″ by 82½″ tall.

The header sits on the two jack studs, so it is 3″ longer than the opening (two 1½″ jacks). Its depth (2×8, 2×10, doubled or not) depends on the span and the load above it: take it from your plans or the lintel span tables in Part 9 of the Alberta building code (the code calls headers lintels).

Formula
header length = RO width + 3″
jack length = RO height − bottom plate (1½″)

For doors the bottom plate is cut out of the opening after the wall is up.

Worked example

32″ door, RO 34″ × 82½″.

  1. Header: 34 + 3 = 37″
  2. Jacks: 82½ − 1½ = 81″ each
  3. Kings: full 92⅝″ studs, nailed to the jacks.
Common mistake

Measuring the RO from the top of the bottom plate. Door RO heights are measured from the subfloor; the plate is later cut out of the doorway.

Walls are usually framed lying flat on the floor. Before sheathing, square the frame by making its diagonals equal (), then tack it to the floor or nail on a temporary diagonal brace so it can’t rack while you sheathe and stand it up.

Worked example

144″ × 97⅛″ wall.

  1. Target diagonal: √(144² + 97.125²) = 173.7″
  2. Diagonals read 174⅛″ and 173¼″: push the long diagonal’s corners together until both read about 173¾″.
Common mistake

Sheathing first and squaring later. The sheathing locks in whatever shape the frame had. Square, then sheathe.

A simple gable roof has pairs of common rafters leaning against a ridge board and resting on the wall’s top plates.

  • Span: outside of wall to outside of wall.
  • Run: the horizontal distance one rafter covers, half the span for a symmetric gable.
  • Rise: how far it climbs over its run. Pitch is rise per 12″ of run ().
  • Overhang: the part of the rafter (the tail) beyond the wall.
Worked example

24′ span at 6-in-12.

  1. Run: 24 ÷ 2 = 12′
  2. Rise: 12 × 6 ÷ 12 = 6′ (to the ridge’s center line, at the outside of the wall)
Common mistake

Using the inside wall-to-wall distance as the span. Span is outside to outside, since the rafter’s bird’s mouth sits at the outside edge of the plate.

The rafter runs along the slope, so its length is the run times the slope factor (). One correction: the rafters stop against the ridge board, not at its center line, so each run is shorter by half the ridge thickness. This is the ridge reduction.

The resulting line length runs from the ridge plumb cut to the plumb line at the outside of the wall. Measure it between those two plumb lines along any line parallel to the rafter’s edge, such as the top edge.

The bird’s mouth is the notch that lets a rafter sit flat on the top plate. It has two cuts: a level seat cut that bears on the plate, and a plumb heel cut that sits against the outside of the wall.

The seat is usually as wide as the wall (3½″ on a 2×4 wall). Don’t notch too deep. The building code limits notches in roof framing to one-third of the member’s depth, and the usual rule for a bird’s mouth matches: leave at least two-thirds of the rafter’s depth above the seat, and never cut past the plate.

Worked example

  2. Depth removed, measured square to the rafter: 1.75 × cos 26.57° = 1.57″, about 28% of 5½″.

Overcutting the corner of the notch with a circular saw (the round blade cuts further on the bottom than the top). Stop at the line and finish the corner with a hand saw.

Cutting tails before the rafters are installed. Many framers leave tails long, then snap a line across all of them and cut them in place so the fascia runs dead straight.

On a hip roof the corners slope too. The hip rafter runs diagonally, at 45° in plan, from the building’s corner up to the ridge; a valley rafter runs along an inside corner where two roofs meet. Because the hip runs diagonally, it covers 16.97″ of run (√2 × 12) for every 12″ that the common rafters cover, while rising the same amount. So its pitch is shallower: “6 in 16.97” for a 6-in-12 roof.

A stair climbs a total rise (floor to floor) in equal steps. Each step has a riser (the vertical height) and a tread (the horizontal depth you step on); the sloping boards that carry them are stringers. The front edge of a tread that overhangs the riser below is the nosing.

Every riser in a flight must be the same height, because your feet expect it. Limits for stairs in a house (the code’s private stairs) under the Alberta building code:

  • Rise: 125–200 mm per step (about 5″ to 7⅞″).
  • Run: 255–355 mm (about 10¹⁄₁₆″ to 14″), nosing to nosing. Each tread’s depth is from the run to the run + 25 mm (1″).
  • Uniformity: neighbouring risers or runs within 5 mm (³⁄₁₆″); tallest and shortest in a flight within 10 mm (⅜″).
  • Headroom: 1950 mm (6′4⅞″) minimum.
  • Width: 860 mm (33⅞″) minimum.
  • Handrail: 865–965 mm (34″–38″) above the nosings; exterior stairs with more than 3 risers need at least one.

A comfort check used by many builders: 2 × riser + tread ≈ 24–25″.

Private-stair limits from Part 9 of the National Building Code – 2023 Alberta Edition. Stairs serving more than one dwelling or a public building have stricter limits. Check with the City of Calgary.

Common mistake

Making the top or bottom step a different height to “use up” leftover rise. It is a classic trip hazard and fails inspection. Divide the total rise evenly.

Start from the total rise, measured from the finished surface below to the finished surface above. Divide by a comfortable target riser (about 7½″) and round up to get the number of risers. Divide the total rise by that whole number for the exact riser height. There is one fewer tread than risers, because the top “tread” is the floor or deck you arrive on.

Formula
risers = total rise ÷ 7½, rounded up
riser = total rise ÷ risers
treads = risers − 1 · total run = treads × tread
Quick stair table (target riser 7½″, max 200 mm ≈ 7⅞″, 10½″ runs)
Worked example

A deck 42″ above the patio, 10½″ treads.

  1. Risers: 42 ÷ 7.5 = 5.6 → 6
  2. Riser height: 42 ÷ 6 = 7″
  3. Treads: 5; total run 5 × 10½ = 52½″
  4. Comfort: 2 × 7 + 10½ = 24½″ ✓
  5. Code check: 7″ = 178 mm (≤ 200 ✓); 10½″ = 267 mm (≥ 255 ✓).
Common mistake

Measuring total rise to the ground right under the deck edge, then building the stair to land 4½ feet out where the ground is lower. Measure the rise at the spot where the bottom step will land (a level and a straight board help).

Every riser is laid out the same height, but the treads add thickness to the steps. At the top, each tread raises its step by its own thickness, and so does the step below it, so the steps between treads stay equal. At the bottom there is no tread under the first step. The first step would therefore be one tread thickness too tall. To fix it, cut one tread thickness off the bottom of the stringer: dropping the stringer.

Worked example

7″ risers with 1½″ (2×6) treads.

  1. Laid out: the first riser is 7″ from the stringer’s bottom cut to the first tread line.
  2. Cut 1½″ off the bottom: the first tread cut sits 5½″ up; with its 1½″ tread it is 7″ above the ground, like every other step.
Common mistake

Forgetting the drop on the bottom, which leaves the first step 1½″ taller than the rest: the step people trip on.

A lathe spins the wood while you hold the tool. This guide covers spindle turning: work where the grain runs along the lathe’s axis, such as table legs, tool handles, balusters, pens and candlesticks. (Bowl and faceplate turning, where the grain runs across the axis, use different tools and techniques and are not covered here.)

Key points

  • Face shield on, sleeves and hair secured, spin the work by hand, start slow, and stand out of the line of fire.
  • Speed: diameter (in) × RPM ≈ 6,000–9,000, capped by your lathe and by any vibration.
  • Ride the bevel and cut downhill, from larger diameter to smaller.
  • Size diameters with a parting tool and calipers; a round taper changes radius by half the change in diameter.
  • The spindle roughing gouge is for spindles only; never use it on bowls or faceplate work.
  • Bed (ways): the rails everything slides along.
  • Headstock: the motor end. Its spindle is threaded for chucks and faceplates, and hollow with a Morse taper (MT1 or MT2 on most small and mid-size lathes) that holds the drive center, a pointed center with sharp spurs that grip the wood.
  • Tailstock: slides along the bed and locks. Its quill advances with a handwheel and holds the live center, a point on bearings that spins with the work.
  • Banjo and tool rest: the banjo slides and locks on the bed; the rest sits in it and supports the tool.
  • Speed control (belt steps or a dial) and often an indexing pin for dividing the work into equal parts.

Two numbers describe a lathe: swing (twice the height from the bed to the center, the largest diameter it turns) and the distance between centers (the longest spindle).

Worked example

A lathe with its centers 6″ above the bed and 20″ apart.

  1. Swing: 2 × 6 = 12″
  2. A 29″ table leg won’t fit; it needs a bed extension or a longer lathe.
Common mistake

Ordering accessories without checking the spindle thread and Morse taper size. Both vary between lathes and are printed in the manual.

The lathe’s hazards are different from a saw’s: the danger is being caught by spinning wood, or hit by wood that breaks or is thrown. Most of it is prevented before you switch on.

Before every start
  • Face shield (not just glasses) and dust protection.
  • No loose sleeves, gloves, ties or jewelry; long hair tied back.
  • Check the blank for cracks, loose knots and bark; reject anything suspect.
  • Tailstock and banjo locked; rest about ⅛″–¼″ from the work.
  • Spin the work by hand one full turn to be sure nothing hits the rest.
  • Check the speed setting is low, then stand to the side of the line of fire and switch on.
While turning
  • Tool on the rest before it touches the wood; keep your hands behind the rest.
  • Stop the lathe to move the rest, to measure with a rule, or to inspect.
  • Swing the rest out of the way before sanding.
  • Never reach over or around spinning work.
Common mistake

Switching on a big, square or out-of-balance blank at the speed used for the last small piece. Make “speed down” the last thing you do before you turn the lathe off.

The right speed depends mainly on diameter: the edge of a big blank moves much faster than a small one at the same RPM. A common rule of thumb keeps the product of diameter and speed between about 6,000 and 9,000. For small spindles that gives more than most lathes can do, so the lathe’s maximum becomes the limit.

Start well below the rule for anything square, long and thin, off-center or suspect, and raise the speed only while the lathe runs smoothly. Vibration means too fast.

Rule of thumb
RPM ≈ 6,000 ÷ D to 9,000 ÷ D

D = largest diameter in inches. Never above your lathe’s maximum; lower for roughing, unbalanced or long thin work.

Spindle speeds (capped at 3,500 RPM)
Worked example

Roughing a 3″ square blank, 18″ long.

  1. Its diagonal is about 3 × 1.414 = 4.2″, so treat it as a 4¼″ diameter.
  2. Rule: 6,000 ÷ 4.25 ≈ 1,400 to 9,000 ÷ 4.25 ≈ 2,100 RPM.
  3. Square and long: start around 800–1,000 RPM, raise once it is round.
Common mistake

Using the flat side of a square blank as its diameter. The corners reach out to the diagonal, and they are what hits the rest and your knuckles.

Choose straight-grained wood without cracks or loose knots, cut a little longer than the finished piece (the ends get marked by the centers). Mark the center of each end by drawing the diagonals.

Taking the corners off first, to make an octagon, makes roughing faster, quieter and safer. Rip them off on the table saw with the blade at 45° () or plane them off.

Formula
corner cut for a regular octagon = side × (1 − 1 ÷ √2) ≈ 0.293 × side

Measured along each face from the corner.

Worked example

A 2″ square blank.

  1. Corner cut: 0.293 × 2 = 0.586″ ≈ ⁹⁄₁₆″ along each face.
  2. On the table saw: blade at 45°, fence set so the cut starts ⁹⁄₁₆″ in from each corner. Four passes.
Common mistake

Marking the center by eye. An off-center blank vibrates, wastes wood and leaves flat spots when it is turned round. Always use the diagonals, and punch the center with an awl.

Between centers: the drive center’s spurs bite into the headstock end, the live center into the tailstock end. Seat the spurs (tap the drive center into the end with a mallet off the lathe, or snug the tailstock firmly), then tighten the tailstock until the work turns only with the drive, and lock the quill. Re-snug it after a few minutes of turning, as the centers bed in.

In a chuck: a four-jaw scroll chuck grips a tenon (spigot) turned on one end. This leaves the other end free for work such as a goblet or the end of a handle. The tenon must match the jaws’ shape (often a slight dovetail; check yours), be a little shorter than the jaws are deep, and have a square shoulder that seats flat against the jaws.

Worked example

A tenon for chuck jaws that close fully at 1⅞″ and are ½″ deep.

  1. Diameter: a little over the fully-closed size, about 2″, so the jaws grip near a full circle.
  2. Length: about ⁷⁄₁₆″, just shorter than the jaws, with a square shoulder.
Common mistake

A long tenon that bottoms out in the chuck before the shoulder seats. The work then wobbles and can pull out. The shoulder, not the tenon’s end, must bear on the jaws.

  • Spindle roughing gouge: a wide, deep U. Rounds square stock to a cylinder fast. Spindle work only: its tang is too weak for bowls and faceplate work.
  • Spindle gouge: a shallower flute with a rounded “fingernail” tip. Beads, coves and detail.
  • Skew chisel: a flat blade with its edge at an angle. Planing cuts that leave a glassy surface, V-cuts and beads. Powerful but prone to catches until you learn it.
  • Parting tool: a narrow blade for sizing cuts and for parting work off. The beading and parting tool is a square ⅜″ version that also rolls small beads.
  • Scrapers: flat bars with a burr. Light finishing cuts, held pointing slightly down.
Worked example

Tools for a simple tool handle, in order.

  1. Roughing gouge: square to round.
  2. Parting tool and calipers: size the ferrule and the grip.
  3. Spindle gouge: coves and the end shape. Skew: a clean planing pass.
Common mistake

Using a spindle roughing gouge on a bowl blank. The cut is interrupted by end grain every half turn, and the tang can snap. Use a bowl gouge for bowls.

The tool rest supports the tool so that the wood’s force goes into the rest, not your hands. Set it parallel to the work, about ⅛″–¼″ away from the highest point, at a height where the tool’s edge meets the wood at or slightly above center when the handle is level or a little low.

As the work gets smaller, move the rest in. A rest far from the work gives the tool leverage against you.

Worked example

Roughing a 3″ square blank.

  1. Its corners sweep a 4¼″ circle, so set the rest ¼″ outside that: about 2⅜″ from the center line.
  2. After it is round at 2¾″, stop and move the rest in to ⅛″–¼″ from the new surface.
Common mistake

Adjusting the rest while the lathe runs. Stop it, move the rest, spin by hand, then restart.

A turning tool cuts cleanly when its bevel (the ground face behind the edge) rubs on the wood just behind the cut. The bevel supports the edge and controls the depth, like the sole of a plane. This is called riding the bevel.

The approach: rest the tool with the bevel touching the spinning wood and the edge not yet cutting, then slowly raise the handle until the edge starts to take a shaving. Move along with the bevel still in contact.

Always cut downhill: from a larger diameter toward a smaller one. That way the fibres you cut are supported by wood underneath them, like planing with the grain, and the surface comes out smooth instead of torn.

Worked example

Turning a cove (a hollow).

  1. Start at the top edge of one side and cut down to the bottom center.
  2. Then cut the other side, again from its top edge down to the center. Never cut up the far wall.
Common mistake

Presenting the edge first, without bevel contact. The edge digs in with nothing to control it: a catch, which can tear the work or pull the tool. Bevel first, then lift the handle to cut.

Most spindle shapes are built from a few elements:

  • Bead: a rounded, convex ring.
  • Cove: a hollow, concave ring.
  • Fillet: a short flat step that separates two shapes and makes them read crisply.
  • V-cut: a sharp groove, made with the point of a skew.

Lay out the positions with a pencil held on the rest while the lathe runs slowly, separate the elements with V-cuts or parting cuts, then shape each one downhill.

Worked example

A ¾″ bead on a 1½″ cylinder.

  1. Mark lines ¾″ apart; mark the center line between them.
  2. V-cut each outer line to about ⅛″ deep.
  3. With the spindle gouge, roll from the center line down into each V, one side at a time.
Common mistake

Cutting away the center pencil line. It marks the top of the bead; leave it until the last pass so both halves come out symmetrical.

Diameters on the lathe are set with outside calipers: two curved legs that you set to the target size against a rule, then hold on the work. The standard technique is a sizing cut: a parting tool in one hand pushes straight in while the calipers, in the other hand, ride in the groove. The moment the calipers slip over, you have reached the diameter.

An open-end wrench of the right size makes a quick go/no-go gauge for common tenons.

Worked example

Sizing a 1¼″ neck on a 1¾″ cylinder.

  1. Set the calipers to 1¼″ against a steel rule (lathe off).
  2. Lathe on at a low speed. Parting tool straight in, calipers lightly in the groove.
  3. Stop when the calipers drop over. Depth of cut: (1.75 − 1.25) ÷ 2 = ¼″ per side.
Safety
  • Use calipers with rounded tips, a light touch and low speed. Sharp tips or a firm grip can catch and pull the calipers around. When in doubt, stop the lathe and measure.
Common mistake

Forcing the calipers over. They spring open and read big, so the part comes out undersize. Let them fall over under their own weight.

  1. Turn the first leg; when you like it, mark its transitions on a stick and caliper each diameter.

Registering the stick from different ends. Mark one end “TOP” and always line it up with the top of the blank.

1¾″ to 1″ over 20″.

Common mistake

Using the full diameter difference as if it were one side: that doubles the angle and makes the foot far too thin.

Chair rungs, legs and handles often end in a round tenon that fits a drilled hole. tenon length = hole depth − about ⅛″
Worked example

A ⅝″ hole drilled 1¼″ deep.

Turning the tenon a hair oversize and hammering it in. Dry wood won’t compress much; it splits the part with the hole. Aim for a push fit by hand.
